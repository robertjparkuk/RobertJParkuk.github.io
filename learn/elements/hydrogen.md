## Climate and carbon

Hydrogen burns without CO₂, so it is promoted for steel, fertiliser, shipping and heavy transport. Its climate value depends on how it is made: most today comes from natural gas without capture. Leaked hydrogen also slows the breakdown of methane, so it warms indirectly. Hydrogen is a good covered by EU CBAM.

## Land, soil and water

Hydrogen is part of every water molecule and every organic compound in soil. Acidity is hydrogen ions: soil pH measures them, and liming removes them.

## Health and safety

Hydrogen gas is not toxic but is extremely flammable over a very wide range of mixtures in air, and its flame is almost invisible in daylight. It leaks through tiny gaps and embrittles some steels. Extremely flammable (H220), burns with an almost invisible flame, leaks easily and embrittles some steels. UN1049 (compressed), UN1966 (refrigerated liquid).

## Industry and supply

Around half of all hydrogen goes into ammonia for fertiliser; most of the rest goes to oil refining and methanol. Electrolysis powered by wind could make it locally, and stored salt caverns could hold it.

## In Northern Ireland

Northern Ireland's wind surplus at night is the case for local electrolytic hydrogen; storage in the Larne salt beds has been studied.

## Remarkable compounds

| Compound | Why it is remarkable |
|---|---|
| Water (H₂O) | the compound every other briefing depends on |
| Hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) | bleach, disinfectant and rocket oxidiser |
| Heavy water (D₂O) | water made with deuterium, used to slow neutrons in some reactors; it tastes faintly sweet |
| Lithium hydride | stores more hydrogen per litre than liquid hydrogen itself |

## Discovery

Identified as an element by Henry Cavendish in 1766; named by Lavoisier from Greek for 'water-former'. Stable.
