## Climate and carbon

Sulfate particles cool the climate; cleaning up coal and ship fuel removed some of that shade. SF₆, the insulating gas in switchgear, is the most potent greenhouse gas in common use.

## Land, soil and water

With far less sulfur falling in rain, silage swards and oilseed rape now often need sulfur fertiliser. Pyrite exposed by digging turns to acid; plasterboard in landfill can make hydrogen sulfide.

## Health and safety

Hydrogen sulfide from slurry mixing kills; it deadens the sense of smell at high levels. Sulfur dioxide irritates the lungs and triggers asthma. See the sulfur briefing safety tab.

## Industry and supply

Sulfuric acid, the most produced industrial chemical, makes fertiliser, batteries and much else. Most sulfur is now recovered from oil and gas.

## Engineering

Sulfur concrete needs no water and resists acid.

## Politics and trade

Clean-air laws and the 2020 ship-fuel cap cut sulfur emissions sharply.

## Commerce

Most sulfur is a by-product of oil and gas refining.

## In Northern Ireland

Slurry is one of the four main causes of death and serious injury on Northern Ireland's farms, HSENI reports.

## Quirks

Brimstone in the Bible is sulfur. Sulfur crystals crack in the warmth of your hand.

## Remarkable compounds

| Compound | Why it is remarkable |
|---|---|
| Hydrogen sulfide | rotten-egg gas from slurry, sewers and bogs |
| Dimethyl sulfide | the smell of the seaside, made by plankton; it helps seed clouds |
| Allicin | the sulfur compound that gives crushed garlic its bite |
| Penicillin | a sulfur-containing antibiotic |

## Discovery

Known since antiquity as brimstone; recognised as an element by Lavoisier in 1777. Stable.
