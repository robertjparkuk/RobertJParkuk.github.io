## Climate and carbon

Phosphorus has no greenhouse gas, but fertiliser making uses energy and phosphorus-rich lakes give off more methane as blooms rot.

## Land, soil and water

The main cause of Lough Neagh's algal blooms. Northern Ireland's farms bring in more phosphorus, mostly in feed, than leaves in food, and the surplus builds up in soil. Soil tests and lime come first.

## Health and safety

Essential for bones and DNA. White phosphorus is very toxic and catches fire by itself in air; red phosphorus on matchboxes is safe. See the phosphorus briefing safety tab.

## Industry and supply

Phosphate rock becomes fertiliser, food additives, detergents, flame retardants and LFP batteries. Phosphorus is on the UK critical minerals list.

## Engineering

Phosphorus recovery from sewage is being built into treatment works.

## Politics and trade

Phosphate rock is concentrated in Morocco and Western Sahara, a disputed territory.

## Commerce

Fertiliser phosphate prices spiked in 2008 and 2022.

## In Northern Ireland

Lough Neagh supplies around two-fifths of Northern Ireland's drinking water. DAERA's new Nutrients Action Programme for 2027–2030 targets the phosphorus surplus.

## Quirks

Discovered in 1669 by an alchemist boiling down urine in search of gold.

## Remarkable compounds

| Compound | Why it is remarkable |
|---|---|
| Apatite | the mineral of phosphate rock, bones and tooth enamel |
| ATP | the molecule that carries energy in every living cell |
| Struvite | phosphorus recovered from sewage as fertiliser |
| Phosphine | a toxic gas used to fumigate grain stores |

## Discovery

Discovered by Hennig Brand in 1669 by boiling down urine, the first element found by a known person; from Greek for 'light-bearer'. Stable.
