The Scopes Map

Scope 1, 2 and 3 as the GHG Protocol defines them, with all fifteen Scope 3 categories. It also covers the grey zones, where the same gas, fuel or vehicle can sit in a different scope depending on who owns it, where it leaks and which gases count.

Your value chain, left to right

Scopes are about who controls the source, not where the gas ends up. Your organisational boundary decides what is "yours". Most firms use operational control: if you run it, it's in. Scope 2 is the only scope defined by what you buy rather than where the emissions happen.

Scope 3 · UpstreamBefore it reaches you
1Purchased goods and services
Cradle-to-gate emissions from making the goods and services you bought in the year, where they aren't counted in categories 2 to 8.
Steel bar, packaging, accountancy, mains water.
2Capital goods
Cradle-to-gate emissions from making capital goods bought in the year, counted in full in the year of purchase rather than spread over their life.
A new CNC machine, a forklift, a building extension.
3Fuel- and energy-related activities
Emissions linked to the fuel and energy you bought that aren't in Scope 1 or 2: extracting, refining and transporting fuels ("well-to-tank"), the upstream side of purchased electricity, and grid transmission and distribution losses.
Gas field methane leaks behind your gas supply; grid losses on your electricity.
4Upstream transportation and distribution
Transport and storage of products you bought, in vehicles and facilities you don't own, plus any third-party logistics you pay for, inbound or outbound.
The haulier you pay to deliver to customers in the Republic.
5Waste generated in operations
Disposal and treatment of your waste and wastewater at facilities you don't own or control.
Landfill methane from your general waste; sewage treatment of your effluent.
6Business travel
Employees travelling for work in vehicles you don't own or operate. Hotel stays are optional.
Flights to Birmingham for the NEC; staff mileage in their own cars.
7Employee commuting
Employees travelling between home and work in vehicles you don't own. Homeworking is optional.
Staff driving in from Larne and Ballymena; home heating on homeworking days.
8Upstream leased assets
Running assets you lease, where those emissions aren't already in your Scope 1 or 2 (this depends on your consolidation approach).
Usually empty under operational control, because a leased unit you run is already in Scopes 1 and 2.

  • Stationary combustion: gas or oil boilers, furnaces, generators
  • Mobile combustion: owned or controlled vans, cars, forklifts
  • Process emissions: chemical reactions, e.g. limestone calcination, enteric methane on a farm
  • Fugitive emissions: refrigerant leaks, SF₆ from switchgear, gas leaks on your side of the meter
Scope 2 · Indirect energyElectricity, heat, steam and cooling you buy Emissions from generating energy you buy and use. The emissions happen at the power station, but they count as yours because you consume the energy.

  • Market-based: reflects your contracts, certificates and supplier fuel mix

Where the lines blur

The questions clients actually ask. In each case the same thing can sit in a different scope depending on ownership, where it leaks, or whether the gas counts at all.

Scope 1Scope 3 · cat 3

Natural gas: burned on site, delivered through a main

Burning gas in your boiler is Scope 1. Methane that leaks before your meter, at the gas field, in transmission or in the local network, belongs to the network operators. It reaches your report only through the well-to-tank factor in category 3. Methane leaking from your pipework after the meter is your Scope 1 fugitive emission, but it's rarely measured.

Test: which side of the meter, and who owns the pipe?

Not countedScope 1 when burned

Butane, propane and LPG leaks

Butane and propane aren't among the seven greenhouse gases the GHG Protocol counts, so a leaking LPG cylinder isn't a reportable emission. It is a safety and air-quality issue. The CO₂ from burning LPG is Scope 1. Propane used as a refrigerant (R290) has a tiny listed GWP of 0.06, so leaks are effectively nil.

Test: is the gas on the counted list? If not, only its combustion counts.

Scope 3 · cat 1Scope 3 · cat 5Scope 1Scope 2

Water

Water itself isn't an emission. Mains water carries the water company's pumping and treatment emissions (category 1), and sewage carries the treatment works' emissions (category 5). Water from your own borehole, rainwater tank or river has no supplier emissions; the energy to pump it is already in your Scope 1 or 2. An on-site treatment plant that releases methane or nitrous oxide is Scope 1.

Water vapour from evaporation or steam isn't counted, because it isn't on the counted list.

Scope 2Scope 3 · cat 3Scope 1 (zero)

Electricity: generated, lost, or made on your roof

The power station's emissions for the electricity you use are Scope 2. The share lost on the grid on the way to you is category 3. Solar or wind you generate and use yourself is reported as zero-emission energy in Scope 1, per DESNZ guidance. If you sell the renewable certificates from it, you can't also claim that electricity as zero-carbon in your market-based figure.

Test: did you buy it, lose it, or make it?

Scope 1Scope 3 · cat 6Scope 3 · cat 8

Vehicles: owned, leased, hired or the employee's own

Owned vans, and leased vans you run, are Scope 1 under operational control. Staff driving their own cars on business ("grey fleet") are category 6, and so are short-term hire cars. Under a financial-control or equity approach, leased vehicles can move to category 8.

Test: who controls how the vehicle is run?

Scope 2Scope 3 · cat 6 / 7

Company EV charged at the office or at home

Charging at your premises puts the electricity in your Scope 2. Charging at the employee's home, with a mileage refund, has no settled treatment. Most firms report it as business travel (category 6), or as commuting (category 7) when the journey is a commute.

Pick one method, write it down and apply it every year.

Scope 1Scope 3 · cat 8 / 11 / 12Reported separately

Refrigerants: yours, your landlord's, your customer's

Leaks from air conditioning or chillers you run are Scope 1, whoever owns them under operational control. Leaks from a landlord-run system serving several tenants can fall into category 8. Refrigerant in products you sell leaks in use (category 11) and at end of life (category 12). Older HCFC gases such as R22 are Montreal Protocol gases, not Kyoto gases, so they're reported separately.

Test: who services it, who sold it, and is the gas on the counted list?

Scope 1Scope 3 · cat 4Scope 3 · cat 9

Freight: your lorry, your haulier, their haulier

Your own trucks burn fuel in Scope 1. A carrier you pay counts as category 4, even for outbound deliveries. Category 9 is only for transport after the sale that you don't pay for. For NI firms shipping to GB or the EU, the ferry leg sits wherever the haulage contract sits.

Test: who pays the carrier?

Scope 1Outside of scopes

Biomass, wood pellets, charcoal and biofuel blends

CO₂ from burning wood, charcoal or the bio-share of diesel and petrol blends is biogenic. It's reported "outside of scopes", not in the totals. The methane and nitrous oxide from the same burning still count in Scope 1, and DESNZ's average biofuel-blend factors already split this out.

Biogenic isn't the same as zero. Disclose it separately.

Scope 1Scope 3 · cat 1

Farm emissions: the farmer's or the processor's?

Methane from cattle digestion, slurry and fertiliser nitrous oxide are process and fugitive Scope 1 emissions for the farm. For a dairy or meat processor buying from that farm, the same tonnes are category 1, and usually the largest part of the footprint. This is why NI agri-food carbon numbers vary so much depending on who reports them.

One tonne, two reports, two different scopes, and that's correct.

Scope 1Scope 2Scope 3 · cat 8

Leased premises and landlord-supplied energy

Under operational control, a unit you lease and run is inside your boundary. Its gas is your Scope 1 and its electricity your Scope 2, even when the landlord pays the bill and recharges you. Shared services you have no control over, such as a landlord's central heating, can go in category 8.

Who pays the bill doesn't decide the scope. Control does.

Scope 1Check the source

Welding gas, CO₂ cylinders and extinguishers

Bought-in CO₂ released on site, such as CO₂/argon shielding gas, carbonation, dry ice or a discharged CO₂ extinguisher, is commonly reported as a Scope 1 fugitive or process emission. Practice varies when the CO₂ was captured from another process. SF₆ leaking from your own high-voltage switchgear is Scope 1, and it has a very high warming potential.

Small volumes, often missed. Check the gas supplier's invoices.

Scope 1Exports not deducted

CHP and electricity you sell

All the fuel burned in your combined heat and power unit is Scope 1, including the share that makes electricity you export or sell to a neighbour. You can't subtract the exported electricity from your scopes; report it separately as information.

Gross in, information out.

Scope 3 · cat 1Scope 3 · cat 2

Goods or capital goods?

The GHG Protocol follows your accounts. If you capitalise it, it goes in category 2, with all its emissions counted in the year you buy it. If you expense it, it goes in category 1. A £4,000 tool can land in either, depending on your accounting policy.

Ask your accountant, not your engineer.

Scope 3 · cat 6Optional uplift

Flights and contrails

A flight's CO₂ is category 6. Contrails and high-altitude nitrogen oxides add warming that isn't counted as a greenhouse gas. DESNZ publishes flight factors "with radiative forcing" to include them. Using them is good practice, and the checker does.

State whether you included radiative forcing.

Which gases count

A greenhouse gas inventory counts seven gases, the Kyoto basket. Each is converted to CO₂ equivalent using its global warming potential. A gas that warms the climate but isn't on this list is reported separately, or not at all.

Counted in Scopes 1, 2 and 3

CO₂Carbon dioxide: combustion and processes
CH₄Methane: gas leaks, landfill, livestock, slurry
N₂ONitrous oxide: fertiliser, combustion, wastewater
HFCsHydrofluorocarbons: most modern refrigerants (R404A, R410A, R134a, R32)
PFCsPerfluorocarbons: aluminium smelting, electronics
SF₆Sulphur hexafluoride: high-voltage switchgear
NF₃Nitrogen trifluoride: semiconductor and panel manufacture

Not counted in the scope totals

Water vapourA real greenhouse gas, but emissions from human activity don't drive its concentration, so it's excluded. Evaporation and steam from your site don't count.
Butane, propaneNot Kyoto gases, so leaks aren't reported. Burning them produces CO₂, which is counted.
HCFCs, CFCsFor example R22. They are controlled under the Montreal Protocol and reported separately if at all.
Biogenic CO₂From biomass and biofuels. Reported "outside of scopes".
Contrail effectsNot a gas. Included only through the optional radiative-forcing uplift on flights.
