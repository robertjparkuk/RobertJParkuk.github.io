How this map was made, and who made it

What is on it. 169 technologies, chosen because people are working on each of them today, in a laboratory, a pilot plant or a working site. They sit in eleven categories and build up in thirteen maps of thirteen, from a core of the most enabling near-term technologies to the frontier ideas that cannot be built yet.

Dot size is a judgement of significance today, from 1 to 5: how much difference the technology could make if it works at scale, weighed against how close it is. A small dot does a smaller or more specialised job; it is not unimportant. Shape shows readiness as found in the sources: commercial, pilot or early commercial, laboratory, or frontier. A gold ring marks a strong Northern Ireland fit: a local developer or researcher, a local resource, or a local problem it addresses.

Links. An arrow from A to B means A makes B more relevant, practical, cost-beneficial or necessary. The links are a judgement, set out so that you can test it and disagree.

Articles. Researched in November 2026 from primary and official sources where they exist: legislation, government departments, regulators, universities and peer-reviewed papers. Company statements and trade press are used only where nothing better exists, and are labelled. Each article lists what is not yet verified. Where a law line says it was checked against the legislation, that is a research check, not legal advice.

Author. Robert J. Park, an independent environmental, carbon and public-affairs consultant in Larne and a chemist by training.
