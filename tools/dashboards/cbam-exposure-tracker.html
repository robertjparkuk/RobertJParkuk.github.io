CBAM Exposure Tracker

Who in Northern Ireland pays a carbon border charge, from when, and at what price. The EU has charged for the carbon in imported steel, aluminium, cement, fertiliser, hydrogen and electricity since 1 January 2026; the UK starts on 1 January 2027. Prices and trade figures as at 22 October 2026; rules checked 11 November 2026.

The short version for NI. The European Commission says that “goods produced and/or released for free circulation in Northern Ireland are not subject to CBAM”. The UK CBAM applies across the whole UK, including NI, from 1 January 2027. The charge NI firms face is on what they import from outside the UK, and that includes goods arriving from Ireland and the rest of the EU.

Why. The CBAM Regulation is not listed in Annex 2 of the Windsor Framework, so, in the Commission’s words, CBAM “does not apply to and in the United Kingdom in respect of Northern Ireland” (CBAM Q&A, questions 7.12 to 7.14, updated 15 October 2026). It could be added only by a UK–EU Joint Committee decision under Article 13(4). EU customers may still ask for your product emissions for their own reporting, and NI groups with an EU company importing CBAM goods are caught on that side.

UK–EU summit, 20 November 2026. An emissions trading agreement would start the process of mutual CBAM exemptions, not finish it: each side needs its own legal act first. Plan for both charges in 2027. A link would exempt EU-made goods, not goods made elsewhere and sold through an EU merchant, so check the origin on the invoice.

Where an NI business stands

Common situations under each regime. A summary of official guidance, not advice: check it against your own goods, commodity codes and routes.

Your situationEU CBAMUK CBAMWhat to do
You make goods in NI and sell them to Ireland or elsewhere in the EUNot subject. The Commission says goods produced in NI “are not subject to CBAM” (Q&A 7.14).Not relevant: these are exports.No EU CBAM declaration covers these goods, so your EU customer does not need CBAM data for them. It may still ask for product emissions for its own reporting (CSRD or Scope 3): that is a product figure, not CBAM data.
You import steel, aluminium, cement, fertiliser or hydrogen into NI from outside the UK and the EUNot subject. Goods released for free circulation in NI are outside the EU CBAM (Q&A 7.14).Liable from 1 January 2027 once CBAM goods pass £50,000 (forward test on any day for the next 30 days; backward test on the first of each month for the last 12 months). Register by 31 January 2028; first return and payment by 31 May 2028.Check commodity codes and import values now. Ask suppliers for emissions verified by a verifier accredited for the UK CBAM, or plan to use default values. Keep records for six years.
You bring steel, aluminium, cement, fertiliser or hydrogen into NI from Ireland or elsewhere in the EUNot relevant: these are Union goods leaving the EU.In scope from 1 January 2027. Finance Act 2026 s.144(5)(a) treats “a Union good that is imported into the United Kingdom as a result of its entry into Northern Ireland” as an import, unless Treasury regulations exclude it, and none do. With no customs declaration, the importer is “the person on whose behalf the good is imported” (s.146(4)), usually you. No jurisdiction is exempt at present.Count your EU purchases from invoices towards the £50,000 threshold: there is no customs declaration to count from. HMRC guidance does not yet cover this case, and some Invest NI material says only goods from outside the UK or EU are caught; the Act is the authority. Take customs advice before January. Ask EU producers for verified emissions and for HMRC’s carbon pricing verification form.
You import fertiliser into NI (as a merchant, co-op or direct buyer)Not subject (goods released for free circulation in NI).In scope from 1 January 2027, including fertiliser from Ireland or the EU. The importer pays, often the merchant or co-op, not the farm. Illustrative 2027 cost: calcium ammonium nitrate, 27% N (CN 3102 40 10), HMRC default 2.120 tCO2e/t × £1.45 = about £3.07 a tonne; ammonium nitrate (3102 30 90), 2.600 × £1.45 = about £3.77 a tonne. Fertiliser defaults carry no UK mark-up.Check the code on your invoice: CAN over 28% N has a different default. Origin decides, not route: fertiliser made outside the EU and bought through an Irish merchant is non-EU goods either way. Live Q1 2027 rates are due by 1 January 2027.
You buy empty cans, kegs, drums, foil, plain tanks or structural steel from outside the UKNot subject (goods released for free circulation in NI).In scope: steel tanks, casks, drums and cans (7309, 7310), aluminium casks, drums and cans (7612), aluminium foil (7607), structures and parts (7308, 7610), bolts and fixings (7318), other steel and aluminium articles (7326, 7616). Out of scope: your filled product, lids and crown corks (8309), plastic kegs, and tanks fitted with cooling or stirring equipment.All CBAM goods count towards one £50,000 test. Ask a customs adviser about racking (7308 or 9403), foil trays (7607 or 7615) and hired or returnable kegs (goods under temporary admission are not imports).
You buy CBAM goods from a UK stockholder or distributorNot subject.You are probably not the importer: the stockholder is, and is likely to pass the cost on in its prices.Check your contracts and Incoterms for who carries the carbon cost. The Incoterms Chooser sets out who does what.
You send goods from NI to the EU for processing and bring them backNot the issue here.Outward processing rules apply. A UK carbon price already paid on the UK-origin goods can reduce the UK CBAM due.Keep the evidence of the UK carbon price on the original goods.
You export goods from NI to the EU and reimport them unchanged within three yearsNot the issue here.Returned goods relief: no UK CBAM, if the goods come back in the same state within three years (Finance Act 2026 s.147(2)(d)). The importer need not be the original exporter.Keep export and reimport records that link the two movements.
Your group has an EU company that imports CBAM goods into the EUThat EU importer is caught. Above 50 tonnes a year (any volume for hydrogen or electricity) it must be an authorised CBAM declarant: customs will not release CBAM goods to anyone else. If it applied by 31 March 2026 it may keep importing while the decision is pending. Certificates from 1 February 2027; first declaration by 30 September 2027 for 2026 imports.Not relevant to the EU import.Check that the EU company is authorised, or applied by 31 March 2026; otherwise it cannot import CBAM goods until it is authorised. Collect supplier emissions data for 2026 imports now.
Goods move from Great Britain to NI, or on through NI to the EUThe Commission answers “Yes” (Q&A 7.14): GB-origin or third-country goods moved through NI to the EU are subject to CBAM when released for free circulation in the EU.GB to NI is an internal UK movement, not an import.Do not route GB-made CBAM goods through NI to avoid the EU CBAM on the strength of this page. EU duty and ‘at risk’ rules apply on entry to NI, Article 27 of Regulation (EU) 2023/956 targets circumvention, and the EU could extend CBAM to NI under Article 13(4) of the Windsor Framework. Take customs advice.
You move CBAM goods from NI to GBNot relevant.Goods made in NI: no UK CBAM (UK origin, s.147(2)(a)). Goods already charged on entry to NI: the Act counts the first importation only (s.144(1)), but HMRC has not confirmed how this works. Other goods that are not qualifying NI goods may be treated as imported on entry to GB (s.144(6)).Take customs advice before January if you move non-UK-origin CBAM goods from NI to GB.

Common misreadings. The first EU declaration is due on 30 September 2027, not in May 2027, and EU certificates go on sale on 1 February 2027, not in January 2026. The UK’s first live sector rates, for January to March 2027, are due by 1 January 2027; this page will add them then. The UK and EU are negotiating a link between their emissions trading systems that could exempt each other’s goods from their CBAMs; until each side’s legal act is in force, both charges apply as set out here.

Default or actual? A worked example

500 tonnes of hot-rolled coil made in Great Britain (CN 7208 10 00).

  • Into the UK, 2027: 500 t × HMRC default 2.722 tCO2e/t (including the 10% mark-up) × £9.69 = about £13,188. Verified data from a plant 20% better than the default saves about £2,638, which may be less than the cost of verification.
  • Into the EU, imported in Q3 2026: on default values the importer owes about €54,588 after the free-allocation adjustment. Verified data from a plant 20% below the unmarked default saves about €29,900 (about €39,800 for 2027 imports), so verification usually pays.

So verified actual data pays where the plant beats the default by enough to cover verification: for EU customers it usually does, because EU defaults carry a 10 to 30% mark-up; for UK imports at today’s illustrative rates it often does not. A plant dirtier than the default should not verify. Figures before any carbon price paid abroad; a verifier’s exact calculation may differ.
