Housing output rose by a quarter. Pipes, timber rules and carbon at the border now set the pace.

Building for a growing Northern Ireland: how contractors, developers and merchants can plan around wastewater limits, imported-materials rules and lower-carbon buildings.

Construction in Northern Ireland has been busy. Output in the first quarter of 2026 was 10% higher than a year earlier, and housing work was up 27%. Output dipped in the second quarter but stayed 17.5% above its pre-pandemic level. The limits now are less about demand than about pipes, materials and rules: wastewater capacity decides where homes can connect, timber and imported steel come with new due diligence and carbon costs, and clients increasingly ask what a building will emit over its life.

Where emissions sitSpread across the inventory

Construction has no line of its own in the NI greenhouse gas inventory. Fuel burned on sites and in cement and quarry plant is counted in Industry: 1.0 MtCO2e in 2024, down 69% since 1990, with cement among the largest long-term cuts. Heating finished buildings is counted in “buildings and product uses” (2.8 MtCO2e, 15.5%). Across the UK, the UK Green Building Council says the built environment is directly responsible for about a quarter of emissions, and around a fifth of that comes from construction and refurbishment rather than running buildings.

Scopes 1, 2 and 3Materials are the footprint

For a contractor, Scope 1 is diesel in plant and vans and fuel in generators. Scope 2 is site and office electricity. Scope 3 is dominated by materials (concrete, steel, aluminium, blocks, insulation, timber), plus haulage and waste. Lower-carbon concrete, steel with recycled content and designing out waste usually cut more emissions than anything done on site.

WastewaterThe real limit on new homes

NI Water estimates that around £7 billion is needed for wastewater capacity, environmental compliance and resilience. In Belfast, 156 of 192 storm overflows are classed as unsatisfactory, and capacity limits stop many proposed homes from connecting.

In March 2026 the Infrastructure Minister announced that developer contributions will start as a voluntary scheme, with a compulsory levy still under consideration. Extra departmental funding since autumn 2024 has unlocked capacity for 5,300 more homes. Developers should check connection capacity before buying land, not after planning is lodged.

Imported materialsTwo rules arrive within eight weeks

UK CBAM. From 1 January 2027, imports of iron and steel, aluminium and cement of any non-UK origin will carry a carbon levy once a business's covered imports pass £50,000 in any 12 months. Rebar, sections, cladding and cement from Turkey, China or India will cost more and come with emissions paperwork, and so will supplies from the Republic of Ireland and the rest of the EU: HMRC says no jurisdiction is exempt, and relief covers only a carbon price actually paid. Only UK-origin goods are outside the levy, and steel bought from a GB stockholder is the stockholder's import, not yours, though expect the levy in the price. For 2027 and 2028, HMRC's default values, used when a supplier cannot provide verified emissions data, are one global figure per good; the 28 October Budget said they become country-specific from 2029. Importers that pass the threshold in 2027 must register by 31 January 2028.

EU deforestation rules. From 30 December 2026, the EU Deforestation Regulation applies in NI to wood and wood products, including some pallets and packaging. The business that first places timber on the NI market, often an importer or merchant, needs due diligence, and buyers need the reference numbers. Micro and small businesses get no extra time for timber: the later date of 30 June 2027 does not apply to wood products already covered by the old EU Timber Regulation, so for them too the date is 30 December 2026.

LandContaminated sites still rely on planning

Part 3 of the Waste and Contaminated Land (NI) Order 1997, which would create a statutory contaminated land regime, has still not been commenced. DAERA's work on the costs of a regime is ongoing, with completion possibly in 2027. Until then, contamination is handled mainly through the planning system, so brownfield developers carry the risk of what they find. A good desk study and early site investigation remain the cheapest insurance.

This monthThree things a contractor or developer can do

  1. Ask your timber and steel suppliers in writing how they handle deforestation due diligence and CBAM, and what documents you will receive with each delivery.
  2. Check NI Water capacity for every site in your pipeline before committing to land or design.
  3. Estimate embodied carbon on one current project: the concrete, steel and timber quantities you already have are enough for a first figure.

Reading these figures with care

  • Construction output is a volume index (2023 = 100), not a value. Figures for 2026 are provisional and will be revised. Q3 2026 figures are due on 14 January 2027.
  • Housing supply. Output measures work done, not homes started. New dwelling starts recorded by building control fell to 1,502 in April to June 2026, 746 fewer than a year earlier; completions were 1,571. July to September figures are due on 18 November.
  • Inventory sectors. The Industry figure includes all manufacturing as well as construction.
  • UK Green Building Council figures are UK-wide, include some consumption emissions and date from 2022. There is no NI equivalent.
  • CBAM and EUDR rules were checked against HMRC, GOV.UK and European Commission guidance on 9 November 2026. If you buy cement or steel from the Republic of Ireland, ask HMRC's CBAM team how that movement will be reported, as it carries no customs import declaration. This is general information, not tax or legal advice: confirm with HMRC or your adviser before you act.
