A £7bn sector, half of it sold to GB. New rules land within twelve months.

Feeding the UK from Northern Ireland: how processors can keep their markets as deforestation, packaging and carbon rules arrive.

Northern Ireland's food and drink processors turned over £6.5bn in 2022 and an estimated £7.1bn in 2023, with almost half of their sales going to Great Britain. Their own emissions are falling: food and drink is one of the main reasons NI industry has cut its emissions by more than two-thirds since 1990. The pressure now comes from customers and new rules. Retailers want supplier carbon data, EU deforestation rules apply from 30 December, packaging fees are in their second year and a deposit return scheme starts in October 2027.

Where emissions sitIndustry has cut deep, and food and drink led the way

In the NI greenhouse gas inventory, factory fuel, industrial processes and industrial refrigeration sit in the Industry sector: 1.0 MtCO2e in 2024, down 69% since 1990. DAERA names food and drink, chemicals and cement as the largest long-term reductions, and lower fuel use in food and drink as one reason emissions fell again in 2024.

That figure is only part of a processor's footprint. Electricity is counted under electricity supply, and the largest share usually sits upstream in milk, meat, grain and feed, which the inventory counts under agriculture.

Scopes 1, 2 and 3The farm is in your footprint

Scope 1 is gas, oil and LPG for boilers, ovens and steam, plus refrigerant leaks. Scope 2 is bought electricity, mostly for refrigeration, compressed air and motors. Scope 3 is everything bought in and sent out: raw materials, packaging, haulage and waste.

For a dairy, meat or poultry processor, raw materials are usually by far the largest part. That links processors directly to the farm figures in last week's Focus on Agriculture: each kilogram of NI milk now carries 40% less carbon than in 1990, and that gain shows up in every dairy's Scope 3.

MarketsCustomers ask before regulators do

Great Britain took 48.5% of sales in 2022, about £3.2bn. The Republic of Ireland took £1.1bn and the rest of the EU £631m. Large UK retailers increasingly ask suppliers for emissions data and reduction targets for their own reporting.

The EU has narrowed its sustainability reporting rules (CSRD): they now apply only to groups with more than 1,000 employees and €450m turnover, and those groups can ask less of suppliers with fewer than 1,000 staff. Most NI processors will still be asked for data, but for less of it.

New rulesDeforestation, packaging, deposits and food waste

  • EU deforestation rules (EUDR). From 30 December 2026, the EU Deforestation Regulation applies in NI to medium and large businesses, and from 30 June 2027 to most micro and small ones. It covers cattle, cocoa, coffee, palm oil, rubber, soya and wood, and products made from them, including some pallets and cartons. The business that first places a product on the NI market needs due diligence. The UK Government's own example is an NI meat processor importing Brazilian beef. Feed mills buying soya meal and confectioners buying cocoa are also in scope. DAERA enforces for cattle, cocoa, coffee, palm oil and soya.
  • Packaging EPR. Year two of Extended Producer Responsibility for packaging is under way. PackUK expects to recover about £1.56bn from producers in 2026/27, with fees recalculated in November.
  • Deposit return. NI's deposit return scheme for drinks containers goes live in October 2027. It covers PET plastic, steel and aluminium containers from 150ml to 3 litres and is run by UK Deposit Management Organisation Ltd. Drinks producers should follow UK DMO guidance on registration and labelling now.
  • Food waste. Under the Food Waste Regulations (NI) 2015, businesses producing more than 5kg of food waste a week must present it for separate collection and must not put it down the drain.

DrinksThree rules meet in the bottle

NI drinks makers face three changes at once, and soft drinks feel all of them.

  • Sugar levy. From 1 January 2028 the Soft Drinks Industry Levy will start at 4.5g of added sugar per 100ml, down from 5g, and will cover milk-based and milk-alternative drinks for the first time. Drinks with 4.5g to 7.9g will pay 19.4p a litre; drinks with more sugar will pay 25.9p.
  • Deposit return. From October 2027, PET plastic, steel and aluminium containers from 150ml to 3 litres carry a deposit. Glass is not in the NI scheme, so the choice of container now affects both cost and paperwork.
  • Packaging fees. Producer fees under packaging EPR are charged by weight and material, so heavy containers carry more of the fee.

A drinks maker that plans reformulation, container choice and packaging fees together will spend less than one that deals with them one at a time.

A nod to Maine

Few NI businesses show how long a local drinks maker can last better than Maine Soft Drinks. The Harkness family began making soft drinks in Ballymena in 1919. John Harkness founded Maine in 1949 and moved it to Ballymoney in 1959, where it remains. Now in its fourth generation, it employs more than 100 people, and its Maine Man vans deliver to over 40,000 homes every week or fortnight, alongside supermarket sales, contract bottling and exports to Great Britain. Raspberryade, Sarsaparilla and Brown Lemonade still come in glass.

A regular doorstep round to tens of thousands of homes is the kind of route that refill and reuse schemes elsewhere are trying to build from scratch.

WaterLough Neagh keeps nutrients in the spotlight

Algal blooms on Lough Neagh have made phosphorus and water quality a public issue. Farm phosphorus surpluses have already fallen by 40% since 1991. Processors are large water users, and those with discharge consents are part of the same picture: effluent quality, water use and cost belong in the same plan as energy and carbon.

This monthThree things a processor can do

  1. List which of your raw materials fall under EUDR (cattle, cocoa, coffee, palm oil, soya, wood) and ask suppliers now for the plot locations and due diligence reference numbers you will need from 30 December.
  2. Check whether you qualify for ESOS on 31 December 2026. If you do, book an assessor early: a compliant audit takes months, not weeks.
  3. Build a simple Scope 1 and 2 baseline from last year's gas, oil and electricity bills, and rank your top ten raw materials by spend as the start of Scope 3.

Reading these figures with care

  • Turnover. DAERA's latest audited figures are for 2022; 2023 figures are provisional. Market shares are for 2022, and the NI share in the first chart is derived.
  • Inventory sectors. The Industry figure covers all manufacturing and construction, not food and drink alone. Electricity is counted separately.
  • EUDR. Scope depends on the product's commodity code. Check the GOV.UK guidance for your products.
