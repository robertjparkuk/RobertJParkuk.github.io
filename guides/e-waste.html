What's inside

A phone, laptop or control panel can contain up to 60 elements, in tiny amounts, bonded together. That mix is why e-waste is valuable and hard to recycle, and why some of it is dangerous.

Precious metals

Small mass, most of the value. Mostly on circuit boards and connectors.

AuGoldPlated contacts, connector pins, chip bonding wire
AgSilverSolder, switches, conductive pastes
PdPalladiumMultilayer ceramic capacitors, plating
PtPlatinumHard-disk platters, sensors
CuCopperNot precious, but often the largest single value by weight: cables, motors, boards

Critical and rare

Hard to source and concentrated in a few supplier countries. Few are recovered today.

GaGallium, germaniumLEDs, chips, fibre optics; subject to Chinese export controls

Poisons

These must be removed or contained before shredding, or they spread into every output.

PbLeadOlder solder, CRT glass
HgMercuryOlder LCD backlights, fluorescent lamps, switches
CdCadmiumNickel-cadmium batteries, older pigments
BrBrominated flame retardantsCasings and boards. Some (PBDEs) are persistent organic pollutants (POPs)
ClPCBs, CFCs and HCFCsOld capacitors; refrigerants and foam in older fridges
BeBeryllium, arsenic, hexavalent chromiumConnectors, gallium-arsenide chips, coatings

The treatment line, and where it's checked

In the UK, discarded electricals have to go to a permitted treatment facility. Approved Authorised Treatment Facilities (AATFs) issue the evidence that producers' compliance schemes pay for, under the WEEE Regulations 2013, enforced in NI by NIEA. The green notes mark the audit points.

1 · Arrival

Weighed and identified

Loads arrive with a transfer or consignment note, a waste code (e.g. 20 01 35* for electricals with hazardous components) and a stream such as large appliances, cooling, displays, small mixed, lamps, or the new vapes stream.

Audit: weighbridge tickets, notes and permitted waste codes
2 · Sorting

Reuse check first

Working or repairable items and valuable parts are pulled out for reuse before anything is broken. Laptops, servers and phones need certified data wiping.

Audit: reuse tonnages; data-destruction certificates
3 · De-pollution

Hazards out by hand

The rules list parts that must come out before shredding: batteries, capacitors with PCBs, mercury parts, CRTs, refrigerant gases and foams, toner, backlit LCD panels, circuit boards, external cables and plastics containing brominated flame retardants.

Audit: batch tests: removed batteries and capacitors per tonne against expected rates (EN 50625)
4 · Shredding

Size reduction

A missed lithium battery can start a fire in the shredder. Shredded circuit boards spread their gold and palladium across many fractions, so boards are usually removed first and sent whole to a precious-metals smelter.

Audit: input logs per batch; fire and incident records
5 · Separation

Into fractions

Magnets take out steel, eddy currents take out aluminium and copper, and density, optical and X-ray sorters split plastics. Plastics with POPs-bearing flame retardants must be separated out and destroyed, not recycled.

Audit: mass balance: tonnes in should equal the sum of tonnes out
6 · Outlets

Smelters, refiners, destruction

Fractions go to steel and aluminium works, copper and precious-metal smelters, plastics recyclers, or high-temperature incineration for hazardous fractions. Every outlet must be permitted, and exports reported.

Audit: downstream destination records; evidence notes; quarterly returns to the regulator

Why the shredder has to know what it shredded

Its permit says what it may accept

Each site is permitted for specific waste codes and quantities. A hazardous load treated as non-hazardous is an offence for both the producer and the operator, and the consignment-note trail shows who knew what.

Contamination travels downstream

One shredded mercury backlight or PCB capacitor contaminates whole batches of metal and plastic, and can make them unsaleable or hazardous. De-pollution has to be proved, not assumed.

POPs plastics must be destroyed

Regulator guidance treats plastics from most small electricals, displays and office equipment as likely to contain POPs flame retardants. They must be separated and destroyed. Large white goods and street lighting are the main exceptions. Putting these electricals in a general skip or the scrap-metal stream is unlawful.

Evidence and money depend on it

The tonnes an AATF reports become evidence that producer compliance schemes buy to meet producers' collection obligations. The figures must reconcile: weighbridge in, fractions out, outlets confirmed. That's why regulators and schemes audit them.

The economics: keep value high, keep parts whole

Recycling recovers the materials. Reuse recovers the materials, plus the energy, labour and carbon that went into making the product. The higher up this ladder an item stays, the more value it keeps.

What an NI business should do with its own e-waste

  1. Keep electricals out of general waste and skips. Store them dry and separate, with batteries taped or removed where safe.
  2. Ask the supplier first. Producers of business (non-household) electricals often have take-back duties, especially when you buy a like-for-like replacement.
  3. Use a registered waste carrier going to a permitted treatment facility, and keep the transfer or hazardous-waste consignment notes.
  4. For IT, insist on certified data destruction with serial numbers. A data breach costs far more than the kit is worth.
  5. Ask for the reuse share and the downstream destinations, not just "100% recycled".
  6. Count it: tonnes by route feed the Waste piece of the Carbon Jigsaw (Scope 3, category 5).

The critical lens: environment versus social

The cheapest "recycling" often happens far from the UK. Electricals exported as second-hand goods can end up stripped by hand and burned for copper at informal dumps in West Africa and South Asia, exposing workers, including children, to lead, mercury and dioxins. That moves the poison rather than recycling it.

A low gate fee and a high "recycling rate" can hide that. A buyer should ask where each fraction ends up, and treat an unusually cheap quote as a reason to check.
