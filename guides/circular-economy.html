The theory

A linear economy takes, makes, uses and throws away. A circular economy keeps products and materials in use at their highest value for as long as possible, and designs waste out from the start. The ideas are older than the label.

1966Spaceship EarthKenneth Boulding: a closed system with finite stocks, as opposed to a "cowboy economy"
1970sIndustrial symbiosisKalundborg, Denmark: one firm's waste heat, steam and gypsum become another's input
1976The loop economyWalter Stahel and Geneviève Reday, for the European Commission: extend product life to save energy and create jobs
1990"Circular economy"David Pearce and Kerry Turner name it in environmental economics
2002Cradle to CradleMcDonough and Braungart: separate technical and biological nutrients
2010sThe butterfly diagramThe Ellen MacArthur Foundation brings it to business and policy
2017The 9R ladderPotting et al.: a ranking from refuse down to recover

Technical loop

Metals, plastics, electronics and machines. They can't safely go back to nature, so they're kept in circulation. The tighter the loop, the more value is kept.

Biological loop

Food, wood, fibres, manure. They can return safely to the soil once their value has been cascaded through uses. This is NI's big circular loop.

How practical is it today?

The global circularity rate is falling while recycling grows, because extraction is growing faster. Recycling isn't the same as circularity: a system can recycle more every year and still become less circular. Here's how it works out in practice, material by material.

Virgin materials are underpriced

The environmental damage from extraction isn't in the price, so recycled material competes with the cheaper option on cost. Carbon pricing, CBAM and EPR start to close that gap.

Quality falls each loop

Most recycling is downcycling: mixed plastics become benches, glass becomes aggregate. Contamination and additives limit how often a material can go round.

Scale and logistics

In a small market such as NI, collecting enough of one material to justify reprocessing is hard. Material crosses to GB, the Republic or further afield, under shipment rules and with transport emissions.

Design is decided upstream

Most of a product's circularity is fixed at the design stage. A recycler can't undo glue, laminates or a sealed battery.

Rebound

Cheaper second-hand goods or more efficient use can raise total consumption, and cancel out the savings.

Keeping old kit isn't always better

Extending the life of an inefficient boiler, compressor or fridge can cost more carbon in energy use than replacing it. Life extension has to be compared with the energy cost of keeping it running.

The road ahead

19 Jul 2026

EU ban on large companies destroying unsold clothing and footwear (Ecodesign for Sustainable Products Regulation). The first ESPR rules on product design, including digital product passports, follow product by product.

31 Jul 2026

EU member states apply the Right to Repair Directive: manufacturers must offer repair for certain products, and consumers get repair information. GB has had more limited right-to-repair rules for some appliances since 2021.

16 Oct 2026

Today. England's Circular Economy Growth Plan is still delayed; it had not been published as of June 2026. NI's final circular economy strategy is still to come. Scotland has its Circular Economy (Scotland) Act 2024.

11 Nov 2026

Expected EU Circular Economy Act proposal: a single market for secondary materials, harmonised EPR, end-of-waste rules, and a WEEE Directive revision covering critical raw materials. Expected date; the proposal has already slipped once.

Feb 2027

EU battery passport for industrial and EV batteries, and removable, replaceable portable batteries. Both apply in NI under the Windsor Framework.

Oct 2027

UK deposit return scheme for PET bottles and steel and aluminium cans, including NI.

2030

EU Critical Raw Materials Act benchmarks: 25% of strategic raw materials from recycling, and no more than 65% from any single non-EU country.

2035

UK Critical Minerals Strategy aims for 20% of demand met from recycling, from wind turbines, EV batteries and electronics.

The critical lens

Circular isn't automatically sustainable. Recycling batteries and electronics needs energy and chemicals and has its own pollution risks. Exporting "secondary materials" to places with weaker controls moves the harm rather than removing it. That's the same environmental-versus-social trade-off seen in mining.

Watch the claims. "100% recyclable" says nothing about whether anything is actually recycled. Under the UK's green claims rules, enforced by the CMA, vague circularity claims are a real risk.

The bigger wins are higher up the ladder. Refusing, reducing and reusing save more than recycling, but they challenge sales volumes. That's why business models such as leasing, take-back and remanufacturing matter more than the recycling rate.

Practical steps for NI businesses and public bodies

  1. Map your top five waste streams by tonnes and cost, and look for any you could sell or reuse rather than pay to get rid of.
  2. Buy for durability and repair: ask suppliers about spare parts, repair manuals, take-back and remanufactured options.
  3. In public tenders, use the 10% social value weighting (PPN 01/21) to reward reuse, repair and local reprocessing.
  4. Redesign packaging to cut EPR fees: less material, mono-materials, recyclable formats.
  5. Look for symbiosis: your waste heat, offcuts or by-products may be a neighbour's raw material.
  6. Measure it: material flows and Scope 3 categories 1, 5 and 12 show where circular changes cut carbon.
