Most of what hangs on a British Christmas tree was invented in Germany, popularised by a royal household, mass-produced in the United States and is now made in China. Almost none of it was designed with fire, carbon, recycling or a border down the Irish Sea in mind. Northern Ireland now sits under two rulebooks at once, so some decorations face a different law in Larne than in Stranraer, and a different one again in Dundalk.

This is a then-and-now of the decorations in our homes, shops, pubs and public spaces: where they came from, what changed, what the rules say today, and where the rules contradict each other. It ends with twelve practical ways to make Christmas better, and a carol for the regulators.

## The decorations, then and now

### Tinsel: from silver to PVC

**Then.** Tinsel is traditionally dated to Nuremberg around 1610, made from strands of real silver that tarnished in candlelight. The date rests on weak secondary sources, so treat it as tradition. By the 20th century much tinsel was lead foil. According to contemporary press reports, in August 1971 the US Food and Drug Administration concluded that lead tinsel was an unnecessary risk to children, and makers agreed to stop *voluntarily* from 1 January 1972. It was never a formal ban.

**Now.** Most tinsel is PVC or polyester film with a thin metallic coat. It cannot be recycled at the kerbside, sheds fibres, and lasts for decades if you keep it. The greenest tinsel is the tinsel already in the loft.

### Baubles: a glassblower's legend

**Then.** The glassmaking town of Lauscha in Thuringia had a ducal licence for its glassworks from 1597. The Corning Museum of Glass tells the story of Hans Greiner blowing glass fruit and nuts for his tree in 1847, when the real thing was scarce, but calls it a legend. Insides were silvered with silver nitrate and sugar. F. W. Woolworth was importing European glass ornaments by the 1890s, and at their peak European imports made up 99% of the 50 to 80 million ornaments sold each year in the US (The Henry Ford). In the war Corning machine-made glass blanks, and metal caps gave way to card.

**Now.** Most baubles are plastic "shatterproof" sets. The UN Comtrade data reported by Statista puts China at about 90% of world exports of "other Christmas decorations" (secondary source; the year is not stated). Christmas decorations are not toys in law: the toy safety rules list "decorative objects for festivities and celebrations" as not toys (my reading; check before relying on it), so they fall under general product safety law. That law has differed between Northern Ireland and Great Britain since 13 December 2024 (see below).

### The tree: from a royal yew to a PVC import

**Then.** The Royal Collection Trust credits Queen Charlotte, George III's German consort, with bringing the custom to Britain in the late 18th century, using a yew. The *Illustrated London News* engraving of Victoria, Albert and their children around a decorated tree in 1848 made it a national habit. The V&A dates the first advertisements for tree ornaments to 1853. Germany made goose-feather artificial trees from the 1880s (secondary sources). The story that the Addis brush company made bristle trees from toilet-brush machinery around 1930 is often told and contested. Aluminium trees arrived in 1958 and faded after the mid-1960s (secondary sources).

**Now.** Most artificial trees are PVC on steel frames, and most are made in China (secondary sources). The British Christmas Tree Growers Association, a trade body, says over 8 million real trees were sold in the UK in 2021 and almost 5 million were "obtained" for 2023. The two figures cannot both describe the same thing, so I do not chart them as a trend.

**Real or fake? Interrogate the evidence.** The most quoted numbers are 40 kg CO2e for a 2 m artificial tree, 16 kg for a real tree sent to landfill and 3.5 kg for one that is chipped. They are attributed to the Carbon Trust, but they are not on the Carbon Trust's own page. They reach us through the growers' trade body, and even the press disagree on whether 3.5 kg means chipped or burned. The Carbon Trust's own advice (29 November 2023) is that chipping cuts a real tree's footprint by up to 80%, and that an artificial tree used for 7 to 20 years generally beats buying a new real tree each year.

Who paid for the study matters too. The two studies that say an artificial tree "wins" after four to nine years (PE Americas, 2010) or five years (WAP Sustainability, 2018) were both paid for by the American Christmas Tree Association, which represents artificial-tree makers. A 2009 Canadian study by Ellipsos put the break-even at about 20 years, and a 2018 review by the non-profit Dovetail Partners at 15 to 20 years on climate alone.

The last two were paid for by the artificial-tree trade body." loading="lazy"></figure>

The honest answer: **keep the artificial tree you already own for as long as it lasts; if you buy real, buy local and get it chipped; never landfill it.** The rules on moving trees have changed too. In 2016 a Scottish fir could cross to a Belfast garden centre needing a plant passport only if it was over 3 m. Today Northern Ireland applies EU plant health law under the Windsor Framework, and DAERA treats the island as a protected zone against the eight-toothed spruce bark beetle (*Ips typographus*), which has broken out in south-east England since 2018. The Northern Ireland Plant Health Label covers *plants for planting*; I could not find official guidance that clearly covers *cut* trees moving from GB to NI. Ask DAERA Plant Health before you import a load.

### Lights: from candles and Edison to LEDs

**Then.** Trees were lit with candles. Edward H. Johnson, an associate of Thomas Edison, hand-wired 80 red, white and blue bulbs around his tree in New York in 1882. In 1900 a 16-bulb string cost $12. In Christmas week 1959, Hansard recorded 14 fires involving decorations in England and Wales: five from naked lights, four from electrical faults, four where decorations fell on fires, and one from soot.

**Now.** LED strings use around 92% less energy than incandescent ones, according to the US EPA's Energy Star programme. In Northern Ireland that matters more, because there is no Ofgem-style price cap (Power NI's tariff is regulated by the Utility Regulator): Power NI's standard rate has been **34.15p per kWh including VAT since 1 July 2026**, against the GB cap of **26.32p** for October to December 2026. That is about 30% more per unit. Part of the gap is VAT: GB domestic electricity is zero-rated from 1 October 2026 to 31 March 2027, but Northern Ireland stays at 5%.

The catch is safety. Cheap LED strings bought online feature in product recalls. Trading Standards recently recalled rainbow pom-pom fairy lights, bought between September and December 2025, as a serious fire risk because they came with the wrong USB cable. Fairy lights fall under the Electrical Equipment (Safety) Regulations 2016. In Northern Ireland the EU Low Voltage Directive still applies under the Windsor Framework, so lights sold here must carry the CE mark and **cannot be UKCA marked** (GOV.UK, updated 27 October 2026). Great Britain accepts either.

### Spray snow: from asbestos to aerosols

**Then.** In the 1930s, fake snow for American homes, churches, schools and film sets was made from asbestos, until the Second World War, when asbestos was needed for US Navy ships (Moffitt Cancer Center). Some frosted vintage decorations still contain it: if a family heirloom sheds white fibres, do not handle it. Brand names such as "White Magic" circulate online, but I found no institutional source for them. Aerosols later used chlorofluorocarbons until the US banned CFC propellants in 1978 and the Montreal Protocol was signed in 1987 (US EPA). I found no source confirming that CFC snow sprays were sold in the UK.

**Now.** Spray snow and flock are aerosols and adhesives. Read the can: many aerosol propellants are flammable, so never spray near lit candles, fires or hot bulbs, and ventilate the room. Fake snow on windows washes into drains, and flock makes paper and trees harder to recycle.

### Glitter: restricted in Larne, legal in Stranraer

**Then.** The New Jersey machinist Henry Ruschmann is credited with cutting plastic film into glitter before 1934, and his firm turned to plastic glitter in the war when glass glitter dried up (local history sources).

**Now.** Commission Regulation (EU) 2023/2055 restricts intentionally added synthetic polymer microparticles, including **loose plastic glitter**, from **17 October 2023**. Defra's own explanatory memorandum (12 October 2024) confirms it **applies in Northern Ireland**. Great Britain has no equivalent: the UK REACH work programme for 2025 to 2026 started no new restrictions, and a Defra answer of 16 March 2026 announced no decision. So a tube of craft glitter that cannot be placed on the market in Larne can still be sold in Stranraer.

There are two further twists. The restriction does not cover glitter **bonded** to baubles, cards or wrapping paper, which is most festive glitter, and is what spoils paper recycling. And "biodegradable" glitter is not the answer it seems: a 2020 Anglia Ruskin University study found that cellulose glitter harmed freshwater plants about as much as plastic glitter.

### Candles: light, heat and carbon monoxide

**Then.** Candles lit the tree until electricity replaced them. The Moravian Church's Christingle dates to 1747, and the Children's Society brought it to British churches in 1968 (secondary sources). The older end of the season was Candlemas on 2 February. English Heritage notes that the rule that decorations must come down by Twelfth Night is a modern invention.

**Now.** Candle labels follow BS EN 15494, with standards for sooting (BS EN 15426) and outdoor candles (BS EN 17616). The NI Direct and Fire and Rescue Service advice (11 December 2025) is simple: never place candles near the tree, decorations, wrapping paper or furnishings, and never leave them burning unattended. I could not extract England's candle fire figures from the Home Office tables, or find official NI Christmas fire figures, so I quote none.

**Carbon monoxide is the hidden Christmas risk.** Candles make little CO. The danger is a fire or stove lit for the first time in months with an unswept chimney, a gas appliance working harder for a full house, or a barbecue or generator brought indoors. Northern Ireland has been ahead here:

- **31 October 2012:** NI Building Regulations require a CO alarm wherever a combustion appliance is installed or replaced in a home.
- **1 September 2024** (new tenancies) and **1 December 2024** (existing tenancies): NI private landlords must fit smoke alarms, a **heat alarm in every kitchen** and a CO alarm wherever there is a fixed combustion appliance (other than one used solely for cooking) or a flue (SR 2024/123).
- **1 October 2022:** England's equivalent covers CO alarms for fixed combustion appliances other than gas cookers, but no kitchen heat alarm.

### Wrapping paper: not packaging, depending on who wraps it

**Then.** People wrapped gifts in brown paper, newspaper or fabric until, the story goes, a Kansas City shop ran out of tissue in 1917 and sold decorative envelope linings instead. That shop became Hallmark.

**Now.** The often-repeated claim that the UK uses 227,000 miles of wrapping paper a year is repeated by Defra (13 December 2024), but it traces back to a packaging company's web page with no published method. Treat it as an industry estimate.

The legal position is odd. The EU list of packaging examples, which UK law mirrors, treats **wrapping paper sold separately as not packaging**, while aluminium foil used to wrap goods at the point of sale is packaging. So a roll of glittered paper sold in a supermarket is outside UK packaging producer responsibility (in force 1 January 2025, including NI) and outside the EU Packaging and Packaging Waste Regulation, which has applied in Northern Ireland since **12 August 2026**. The same paper used by a shop to gift-wrap your purchase at the till is probably packaging. The rule depends on who does the wrapping. Use the scrunch test: if the paper stays scrunched, it can usually go in the recycling; if it springs back, it is plastic or foil and cannot.

### Your turkey foil is a carbon border good

Here is the twist few people know. Aluminium foil (commodity code 7607) is in the EU carbon border adjustment mechanism (CBAM), which entered its definitive phase on **1 January 2026**. EU importers bringing in under 50 tonnes a year are exempt (Regulation (EU) 2025/2083 of 8 October 2025, in force 20 October 2025). Foil is also in scope of the **UK CBAM from 1 January 2027**, which applies across the whole UK, including Northern Ireland, once covered imports pass, or are expected to pass, **£50,000** (over the past 12 months or the next 30 days) (HMRC, updated 2 November 2026).

The steel in a tree stand is less clear. For now, the EU CBAM covers "other articles of iron or steel" (7326) but not household articles (7323); a Commission proposal of December 2025 would extend it to downstream steel and aluminium goods from 2028 (not yet checked against EUR-Lex). Two near-identical stands can sit on either side of that line, depending on how they are classified. That is a question for a customs specialist, not a blog.

## Retail, hospitality, leisure and the public realm

Decorations are a business, and a public-realm job.

- **Markets and events.** Belfast City Council reports over a million visitors to the 2024 Christmas market, with over 100 stalls, 40% of them local. The lights switch-on (16 November 2024) drew over 10,500 people and an estimated £195,585 in direct spend. A temporary street trading licence in Belfast now costs £244 to apply, plus £54 to £95 a day (fees from 1 June 2026).
- **Fire safety in premises.** Pubs, shops, halls and churches open to the public need a fire risk assessment under the Fire and Rescue Services (NI) Order 2006 and the Fire Safety Regulations (NI) 2010 (in operation 15 November 2010). The GOV.UK guide for places of assembly (written for England and Wales, but a useful reference) lists decorations as a fuel and asks for fire-retardant display materials. That sets safety against recycling: retardant treatments make decorations harder to recycle.
- **Working at height.** Bury Council was fined £200,000 on 2 April 2025 after a man fell while putting up the town's Christmas tree (HSE). HSE says heavy festive displays need secure anchor points, and lights across highways need extra checks.
- **Litter.** A council in Northern Ireland can set a fixed penalty of up to **£200** for dropping litter under the Litter (NI) Order 1994 (SR 2022/302, from 30 December 2022). In England the maximum has been **£500** since 31 July 2023. Sky lanterns and balloon releases are left to individual councils: Armagh City, Banbridge and Craigavon has consulted on a ban, and I found no NI-wide position.

## One decoration, three rulebooks

This is where duplication becomes contradiction. The same product can be lawful, restricted or unregulated depending on which side of the Irish Sea or the border it is sold.

| Issue | Northern Ireland | Great Britain | Republic of Ireland / EU |
|---|---|---|---|
| Loose plastic glitter | Restricted since 17 Oct 2023 (EU 2023/2055) | Legal; no restriction decided (16 Mar 2026) | Restricted since 17 Oct 2023 |
| General product safety | EU GPSR 2023/988 since 13 Dec 2024 | GPSR 2005; new framework consulted on, Mar–Jun 2026 | EU GPSR since 13 Dec 2024 |
| Fairy lights marking | CE only, not UKCA | UKCA or CE | CE |
| Packaging design and labels | EU PPWR from 12 Aug 2026, plus UK packaging EPR | UK packaging EPR | EU PPWR from 12 Aug 2026 |
| Generic "eco" claims | Case by case, DMCC Act (from 6 Apr 2025) | Case by case, DMCC Act | EU rule from 27 Sep 2026 bans them unless proven (Directive 2024/825)*; Irish transposition not checked |
| Maximum litter fine | £200 (since 30 Dec 2022) | £500 in England (since 31 Jul 2023) | Not checked |
| Aluminium foil (CBAM) | UK CBAM from 1 Jan 2027 | UK CBAM from 1 Jan 2027 | EU CBAM, definitive since 1 Jan 2026 |
| Electricity price | Power NI 34.15p/kWh incl. 5% VAT (regulated tariff) | Ofgem cap 26.32p/kWh, VAT zero-rated to 31 Mar 2027 | Not compared |

*I could not find an official statement on whether Directive 2024/825 applies in Northern Ireland. As far as I can tell, the underlying consumer directives are not in Annex 2 of the Windsor Framework, so it does not. Confirm before relying on it.

### Where the ESG pillars collide

ESG is usually sold as if environmental, social and governance goals line up. At Christmas, they visibly don't.

- **LEDs** save energy and money (E), but cheap imported strings are the ones being recalled for fire risk (S).
- **Fire-retardant decorations** protect people in pubs and halls (S), but the treatments make them harder to recycle (E).
- **Real trees** support local growers and jobs (S) and store carbon while growing, but they are farmed with inputs and need careful disposal (E). **Artificial trees** avoid pests and annual felling but are PVC and steel shipped from China.
- **The glitter rule** bans the loose craft tube but exempts the glitter bonded to cards and wrap, which does the most damage to recycling.
- **Treat the evidence with care.** The two studies paid for by the artificial-tree industry give the shortest break-even times; the figures repeated by the real-tree industry could not be traced to the source they name. Funding is a declared interest, not proof of error, but anyone selling a "green" Christmas should show their working. That is where duplication can shade into duplicity: a "biodegradable" glitter that harms rivers like plastic, or an "eco" label with nothing behind it.

## Twelve ways to make Christmas better

Tagged by who gains: **£** pocket, **J** jobs, **E** energy, **Env** environment, **C** Christianity.

1. **Switch to LEDs and put them on a timer** (£, E). A small LED string costs pennies a season; an old C9 string costs over £14 at NI prices.
2. **Keep the tree you have.** If it is artificial, use it until it falls apart. If you buy real, buy Northern Ireland-grown and have it chipped (Env, J).
3. **Pass the scrunch test.** Choose plain paper, brown paper or fabric wraps; skip foil and glitter (Env, £).
4. **Buy decorations from local makers and markets**, not anonymous online sellers (J). Belfast's market is 40% local traders.
5. **Check before you plug in.** Buy lights from retailers you can find again, look for the CE mark in NI, check the GOV.UK product recalls database, and unplug at night (£, safety).
6. **Test your alarms and sweep the chimney** before the first fire of the season. Fit a CO alarm wherever there is a fire, stove or boiler (safety).
7. **Treat candles with respect.** Never leave them unattended; use LED candles in windows and near greenery (safety, C).
8. **Councils: plan the after-party.** Collect and chip trees, publish dates early, ban lantern and balloon releases on council land, and use the full litter fine range (Env, public realm).
9. **Hospitality: update the fire risk assessment** for the decorations, keep exits clear, and buy from local suppliers (J, safety).
10. **Retailers: make honest green claims.** The CMA can act directly under the DMCC Act from 6 April 2025, and the rules are stricter across the border in the Republic (£, governance).
11. **Keep the twelve days, and the meaning.** Christmas starts on 25 December and runs to Twelfth Night, with Epiphany on 6 January. Go to a carol service, support a church food bank or a Christingle, and leave the greenery up until Twelfth Night, or Candlemas if you are a traditionalist (C, J).
12. **Buy less, buy better.** One glass bauble that lasts fifty years beats a plastic set a season. Mend lights, reuse tins and boxes, and give experiences (£, Env).

## The twelve days of regulation

*To the tune of "The Twelve Days of Christmas", first printed in* Mirth Without Mischief *in 1780. Each verse names a rule, my verdict, and a tip.*

1. **On the first day of Christmas, the EU gave to me: a ban on loose plastic glitter.** *Good, but half done.* It applies in NI from 17 October 2023 but not in GB, and bonded glitter is exempt. *Tip:* skip glitter; "biodegradable" glitter isn't harmless either.
2. **On the second day of Christmas, Stormont gave to me: two alarms and a heat one.** *Good.* NI tenancies need smoke, heat and CO alarms (from 1 December 2024 for existing tenancies). *Tip:* press the test button before the guests arrive.
3. **On the third day of Christmas, Westminster gave to me: two marks for fairy lights.** *Bad: duplication.* Lights sold in NI must be CE marked and cannot be UKCA marked; in GB either will do, so a GB seller shipping to Larne can get it wrong. *Tip:* in NI, look for CE, and check the recalls database.
4. **On the fourth day of Christmas, my council gave to me: a £200 litter fine.** *Better than it was, but still below England's £500, and rarely used.* NI raised it from £80 on 30 December 2022. *Tip:* councils should use the full range and publish tree collection dates.
5. **On the fifth day of Christmas, HMRC gave to me: a carbon tax on foil.** *Good in principle, odd in practice.* UK CBAM covers aluminium foil from 1 January 2027 above £50,000 of imports. *Tip:* importers, track your 12-month total now; households, wash and ball foil for recycling.
6. **On the sixth day of Christmas, DAERA gave to me: a beetle-free protected zone.** *Good.* The island keeps *Ips typographus* out. *Tip:* buy an NI-grown tree, and ask DAERA before bringing cut trees over from GB.
7. **On the seventh day of Christmas, the CMA gave to me: fines for fibbing.** *Good.* Direct consumer enforcement from 6 April 2025. *Tip:* if you sell "eco" decorations, hold the evidence before you print the label.
8. **On the eighth day of Christmas, the Fire Service gave to me: a risk assessment.** *Good.* Required for premises under the 2010 NI Regulations. *Tip:* add the decorations, check the exits, and keep candles away from foliage.
9. **On the ninth day of Christmas, Brussels gave to me: a packaging rule with a gift-wrap hole.** *Bad: loophole.* Wrapping paper sold on a roll is not packaging. *Tip:* scrunch test; wrap in brown paper or fabric.
10. **On the tenth day of Christmas, the market gave to me: 34p a unit and no GB cap.** *Bad: NI pays about 30% more than GB, partly because GB's VAT on electricity is zero until March.* *Tip:* LEDs on a timer can cut lighting costs by 90% or more against old bulbs.
11. **On the eleventh day of Christmas, the HSE gave to me: a fine for a fallen tree-man.** *Good, sadly.* Bury Council, £200,000, April 2025. *Tip:* councils and venues, use competent contractors and anchor heavy displays.
12. **On the twelfth day of Christmas, nobody gave to me: a ban on sky lanterns.** *Bad: a gap.* There is no NI-wide rule; it is left to councils. *Tip:* never release lanterns or balloons; councils should ban them on their land.

## What I'd change

Three fixes would remove most of the duplication. **First**, the UK should decide on loose glitter, so Larne and Stranraer follow the same rule. **Second**, gift wrap and seasonal decorations should come into producer responsibility on recyclability, or at least carry the scrunch test on the label. **Third**, Northern Ireland's councils should agree one festive public-realm standard: tree collection and chipping, a lantern and balloon ban, and litter enforcement that matches England's.

None of this needs a no-fun Christmas. It needs rules that say the same thing on both sides of the Irish Sea, and decorations made to last.
