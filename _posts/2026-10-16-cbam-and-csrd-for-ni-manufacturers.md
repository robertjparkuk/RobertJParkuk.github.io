Four things have moved since this post first went up:

- **UK–EU emissions trading link.** The Council of the EU authorised linking talks in November 2025, and on 10 November 2026 its working party on the UK discussed a [draft linking agreement](https://data.consilium.europa.eu/doc/document/CM-4531-2026-INIT/en/pdf). No text has been published. The Prime Minister told the Commons on 12 October that the link was on the table for the UK–EU summit in Brussels on 20 November. Even a signed deal would not exempt anyone straight away: each side's CBAM exemption needs its own legal act afterwards. Plan for both CBAMs applying in 2027, and treat an exemption as upside.
- **UK CBAM detail.** The regulations on registration, rates, carbon price relief and emissions verification were made between July and September. On 16 October HMRC published [illustrative 2027 rates](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/illustrative-cbam-rates-and-worked-example-for-carbon-border-adjustment-mechanism-cbam/carbon-border-adjustment-mechanism-cbam-illustrative-cbam-rates-and-worked-example) and [draft default values](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/check-the-default-values-for-the-carbon-border-adjustment-mechanism-cbam) for all five sectors: iron and steel £9.69, aluminium £9.79, cement £8.78, fertiliser £1.45 and hydrogen £9.24 per tonne of CO2e. Default values apply in 2027 and 2028 with a 10% mark-up (none for fertiliser) and do not use EU data; they become country-specific from 2029. HMRC publishes each quarter's rates by the start of that quarter, so live rates for January to March 2027 are due by 1 January 2027. Charging for indirect emissions is put off until at least 2029.
- **Northern Ireland and the EU CBAM.** The European Commission's [CBAM Q&A](https://taxation-customs.ec.europa.eu/document/download/013fa763-5dce-4726-a204-69fec04d5ce2_en?filename=CBAM_Questions+and+Answers.pdf) (updated 15 October 2026) says: "CBAM is not listed in Annex 2 of the Windsor Framework… Therefore, CBAM does not apply to and in the United Kingdom in respect of Northern Ireland", and "Goods produced and/or released for free circulation in Northern Ireland are not subject to CBAM" (Q 7.12 and 7.14). The UK CBAM applies in Northern Ireland from 1 January 2027, including to steel, aluminium, cement, fertiliser and hydrogen brought in from Ireland or the rest of the EU (Finance Act 2026, s.144(5)(a)). HMRC has not yet published guidance on how those movements, which carry no customs declaration, are captured.

If you make things in Northern Ireland and sell them into Great Britain, the Republic or further into Europe, you've probably had an email in the last year asking about your carbon. Sometimes it's a customer's procurement team. Sometimes it's a questionnaire that seems to have been written for a multinational. Either way, the acronyms doing the asking are usually CBAM and CSRD.

Here's my plain-English take on both, and why they matter more here than almost anywhere else in the UK.

## CBAM in one paragraph

A Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism puts a carbon price on certain imported goods, so that products made where carbon is cheap don't undercut products made where it isn't. The EU's version moved into its definitive phase on 1 January 2026. It covers iron and steel, aluminium, cement, fertilisers, hydrogen and electricity. Importers bringing in more than 50 tonnes a year of the covered goods need to be authorised CBAM declarants, buy certificates from February 2027, and file their first annual declaration by 30 September 2027 for goods imported during 2026.

The UK is following with its own CBAM from 1 January 2027. It covers aluminium, cement, fertilisers, hydrogen, and iron and steel, and it bites on importers once the value of covered goods reaches £50,000. The first UK return and payment, for the whole of 2027, is due by 31 May 2028.

## Why Northern Ireland is a special case

Northern Ireland sits between the UK and EU systems in a way nowhere else does. The European Commission says the EU CBAM does not apply in respect of Northern Ireland, because the CBAM Regulation is not listed in Annex 2 of the Windsor Framework. Adding it would need a decision of the UK–EU Joint Committee, and none has been proposed. Meanwhile the UK and EU are negotiating a link between their emissions trading schemes, which could lead to mutual exemptions from each other's CBAM. That deal isn't done yet.

So the honest answer to "does CBAM apply to me?" depends on where your goods are made and where you buy from:

- **You make steel, aluminium, cement or fertiliser products in Northern Ireland and sell them into the EU.** Your EU customer has no CBAM declaration to make on them: the Commission says goods produced in NI are not subject to CBAM. Your customer may still want your figures for its own carbon reporting. If you do only minor processing in NI on metal made in Great Britain or elsewhere, whether your goods count as "produced in NI" is an origin question for a customs specialist.
- **You supply the same goods into the EU from Great Britain.** Your EU customer is the importer, and its CBAM declaration needs your embedded emissions data. If you use steel or aluminium made in the EU, the Commission's Q&A (Q 4.18) says its embedded emissions count as zero, so verified data can cut your customer's CBAM cost to a fraction of the default value. Keep mill certificates that prove EU origin.
- **You import covered goods into the UK, including into Northern Ireland from Ireland or the rest of the EU.** The UK scheme reaches you from 1 January 2027 once your covered imports pass £50,000.

## CSRD has shrunk, but it hasn't gone away

The Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive was originally going to pull in tens of thousands of EU companies. The 2025–26 "Omnibus" simplification cut that back hard. Mandatory reporting now applies only to companies with more than 1,000 employees and more than €450 million turnover, plus large non-EU groups with significant EU sales.

That sounds like it lets small and mid-sized NI firms off the hook. Directly, it does. But your large EU customers are still in scope, and they still need value-chain data. The good news is that the Omnibus added a cap: suppliers with up to 1,000 employees can't be pushed beyond a defined, voluntary-standard level of information. You have the right to say no to requests beyond that.

## What I'd do now

- **Map your exposure.** List the products you sell into the EU and the goods you import. Check which fall into CBAM categories, on either side.
- **Talk to your top five customers.** Ask what data they'll need and when. It's far better to find out now than in a tender.
- **Start a carbon inventory.** Embedded emissions data comes from the same place as a carbon footprint: your energy, your materials, your processes. But a product footprint or Environmental Product Declaration is not CBAM data. CBAM counts only the emissions inside set system boundaries, calculated and verified its own way, so the same source data gives a different number.
- **Keep it proportionate.** You don't need a 200-page sustainability report. You need credible numbers and a clear story behind them.

None of this has to be a box-ticking exercise. Done properly, the work of answering these questions tells you a lot about your costs, your suppliers and where your business is exposed. That's the bit I find most useful, and it's what the rest of this series is about.
