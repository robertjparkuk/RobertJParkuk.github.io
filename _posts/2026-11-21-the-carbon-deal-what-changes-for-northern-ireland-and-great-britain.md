*Status as at 20 November 2026. General information, not legal, tax or customs advice.*

On 20 November 2026 the UK and the EU concluded talks to link their carbon markets, aiming for 1 January 2028. Nothing changed in law on the day. But the papers published with it carry one line that matters a great deal here: the UK government says it "intends to exempt goods originating in the EU" from the UK's new carbon border charge from 1 January 2027 ([DESNZ explainer](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/uk-eu-linking-agreement-explainer/uk-eu-linking-agreement-explainer)). If that is legislated, Northern Ireland firms buying EU-made steel, cement or fertiliser are spared the charge from day one. Great Britain's exporters wait a year longer for the EU to return the favour.

## Two systems, in plain words

- An **emissions trading scheme (ETS)** charges producers at home for the carbon they emit. The UK has run its own since leaving the EU's in 2021.
- A **carbon border adjustment mechanism (CBAM)** charges importers for the carbon in goods made abroad: iron and steel, aluminium, cement, fertiliser and hydrogen. The EU's has charged since January 2026. The UK's starts on 1 January 2027, across the whole UK.

Northern Ireland sits in both worlds. Its power stations stay in the EU ETS because they sell into the all-island electricity market. Its industry is in the UK ETS. The EU CBAM does not apply in respect of NI, so goods made here are outside it.

## Then, now, 2027 and 2028

| | Then (2021–25) | Now (2026) | 2027 | From 2028 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Carbon markets** | Separate. Prices diverge | UK near £60 a tonne, EU near €82 (Reuters) | Laws passed on both sides. Prices likely to converge | Linked. One price in practice. The UK follows EU rules, with a say but no vote |
| **EU-made goods into NI or GB** | No charge | No charge | UK says it intends to exempt them. Not law yet | Exempt, if regulations are made |
| **GB-made goods into the EU** | Reporting only | EU buyers owe the EU CBAM | EU buyers still owe it | Exempt, expected, once the EU legislates |
| **NI-made goods into the EU** | Outside the EU CBAM | Outside | Outside | Outside. The edge over GB ends |
| **Goods made outside the UK and EU** | No charge | EU CBAM only | Both charges apply | Both charges apply |

The aim of 1 January 2028 is in the [Joint Statement](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/ukeu-summit-2026-key-documents). The treaty text is not yet published.

## Where it was made is what counts

The charge follows origin. Turkish steel bought from a stockholder in Dublin or Liverpool is still Turkish steel. Fertiliser blended or bagged in Ireland may have been made outside the EU. A mill certificate or a written origin statement, line by line, is now worth money.

## What it means by type of business

- **Steel fabricators.** At HMRC's illustrative 2027 rate, steel made outside the UK and EU carries roughly £26 to £37 a tonne on default values for hot-rolled coil. Buy through a GB stockholder and they are the importer; you see a surcharge. Buy across the border from Ireland and you are probably the importer yourself, with no customs paperwork behind you. First payment is due by 31 May 2028.
- **Engineering and machinery makers.** Finished machines are not CBAM goods. Fabricated steelwork, tanks, tubes and fasteners are. NI makers of those keep an edge over GB rivals into the EU until 2028. After that the selling point is your carbon data, which customers will keep asking for.
- **Builders' merchants.** Cement, rebar, sections, tube and fixings are on the list. Bringing them from Ireland can pass the £50,000 registration test quickly. EU-made lines should drop out; Turkish rebar bought through an Irish wholesaler will not.
- **Farms and merchants.** Farming is in neither ETS and food is not a CBAM good. Fertiliser is. At 2027 rates the charge on non-EU CAN is about £3 a tonne, under 1% of the price. It rises steeply as free allowances for UK producers are withdrawn. Nothing in the deal puts a carbon price on imported food, so the farming unions' leakage argument is untouched.
- **Food and drink processors.** Empty cans, foil, drums and stainless tanks are CBAM goods. Larger sites with an ETS permit should budget for the EU carbon price, not the UK's.
- **GB exporters.** Your EU customers owe the EU CBAM on 2026 and 2027 goods. Keep supplying the data.

## The case for and against

**For.** A larger, more liquid carbon market. No border charge or data burden on UK–EU trade once both exemptions apply. For NI, the oddity of a UK tax on goods from across the border should go, and NI power and NI industry face the same carbon price.

**Against.** The UK follows EU carbon-market rules without a vote and gives up its own price floor. UK sites in the ETS are likely to pay about £10 a tonne more at today's prices. NI industry pays more for carbon with no matching fall in power costs, because NI generators already pay the EU price. And NI loses its niche as the one part of the UK outside the EU CBAM.

It is also contested. Labour would keep the UK ETS and CBAM. The Conservatives and Reform UK have pledged to abolish them (press reports). Ending the UK ETS would lift a cost from every UK site, and would also put GB-made goods back into the EU's border charge. Firms should plan for either outcome.

## It will not fix Northern Ireland's carbon budget

Agriculture is 31.4% of NI emissions and 10% above its 1990 level. Transport is 21.3% and buildings 15.5%. None is in an ETS. Power and industry, the sectors the deal touches, are about a sixth. The 2028–32 budget asks for an average cut of 48%, and the Climate Action Plan is still not laid. This agreement does not change that sum.

## What to do now

1. List every supplier of steel, aluminium, cement or fertiliser and mark each as GB, Irish or other.
2. Ask each one, for every invoice line, where the goods were made and the 8-digit commodity code.
3. If these goods cross the border from Ireland to you, add up what you expect to buy that way in 2027. Over £50,000, assume you may have to register with HMRC by 31 January 2028.
4. Keep the records from 1 January 2027: code, date, value, weight, origin and evidence for any exemption.
5. Treat the exemption for EU-made goods as likely, not as law. HMRC's guidance still said on 2 November that no country is exempt.
