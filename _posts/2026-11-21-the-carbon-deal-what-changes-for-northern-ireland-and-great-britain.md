*Status as at 20 November 2026. General information, not legal, tax or customs advice. The summit papers are political statements and a government guide, not law; take advice on your own goods and routes before you act.*

On 20 November 2026 the UK and the EU concluded talks to link their carbon markets, aiming for 1 January 2028. Nothing changed in law on the day. But the papers published with it carry one line that matters a great deal here: the UK government says it "intends to exempt goods originating in the EU", the wider European Economic Area and Switzerland from the UK's new carbon border charge from 1 January 2027 ([DESNZ explainer](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/uk-eu-linking-agreement-explainer/uk-eu-linking-agreement-explainer)). If that is legislated, Northern Ireland firms buying steel, cement or fertiliser of EU origin are spared the charge from day one. Great Britain's exporters wait a year longer for the EU to return the favour.

## Two systems, in plain words

- An **emissions trading scheme (ETS)** charges producers at home for the carbon they emit. The UK has run its own since leaving the EU's in 2021.
- A **carbon border adjustment mechanism (CBAM)** charges importers for the carbon in goods made abroad: iron and steel, aluminium, cement, fertiliser and hydrogen. The EU's has charged since January 2026. The UK's starts on 1 January 2027, across the whole UK.

Northern Ireland sits in both worlds. Its power stations stay in the EU ETS because they sell into the all-island electricity market. Its larger industrial sites, those with 20MW or more of combustion plant, are in the UK ETS. The EU CBAM does not apply in respect of NI, so goods made here and moved direct into the EU are outside it. Goods from Great Britain or elsewhere that only pass through NI do not qualify.

## Then, now, 2027 and 2028

| | Then (2021–25) | Now (2026) | 2027 | From 2028 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Carbon markets** | Separate. Prices diverge | UK near £60 a tonne, EU near €82 (Reuters) | Laws due on both sides; none laid yet. Prices likely to converge | Linked, if ratified. One price in practice. The UK follows EU rules, with a say but no vote |
| **EU-origin goods into NI or GB** (EEA and Swiss too) | No charge | No charge | UK says it intends to exempt them. Not law yet | Exempt, if regulations are made |
| **GB-made goods into the EU** | Reporting only | EU importers owe the EU CBAM | EU importers still owe it | Exempt, expected, once the EU legislates |
| **NI-made goods moved direct into the EU** | Outside the EU CBAM | Outside | Outside | Outside. The edge over GB ends |
| **Goods from anywhere else** | No charge | EU CBAM, if imported into the EU | Charged by whichever system they enter: UK CBAM into the UK, EU CBAM into the EU. Goods cleared in the EU and then moved into NI can meet both | Same |

The [Joint Statement](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/ukeu-summit-2026-key-documents) says "from 2028"; 1 January 2028 is the aim given in the government's explainer. Both are political documents, not law, and the treaty text is not yet published.

## Origin is what counts

The charge follows origin, and origin has a legal meaning. HMRC uses the UK's non-preferential rules of origin: broadly, where the goods were wholly produced or last substantially processed. That is not always where the metal was first melted. Steel rolled in an EU mill from imported slab may well count as EU-origin; steel that was only cut or stocked in the EU will not. Turkish rebar bought from a stockholder in Dublin or Liverpool is still Turkish rebar. Fertiliser blended or bagged in Ireland may or may not count as EU-origin, and HMRC has not said. The regulations that would create the exemption are not published and may set their own test. A written statement of origin, line by line, with the mill certificate behind it, is now worth money.

## What it means by type of business

- **Steel fabricators.** At HMRC's illustrative steel rate of £9.69 per tonne of CO2e, chargeable steel carries roughly £24 to £45 a tonne on HMRC's default values, depending on the commodity code: about £24 to £45 for sections, £25 to £42 for plate and £26 to £37 for hot-rolled coil. Bought-in fabricated steelwork is a CBAM good too, at about £48 to £63 a tonne. Buy from a GB stockholder's cleared stock and they are the importer; you see a surcharge. Buy goods across the border from Ireland and you may well be the importer yourself, with no customs declaration behind you: your invoices and delivery notes are the record. If a GB merchant has goods delivered to you direct from Ireland or the EU, ask who the importer is. For 2027 the return and payment are due by 31 May 2028, with three more quarterly payments by 30 November 2028.
- **Engineering and machinery makers.** Finished machines are not CBAM goods. Fabricated steelwork, tanks, tubes and fasteners are. NI makers of those keep an edge over GB rivals into the EU until 2028. After that the selling point is your carbon data, which customers will keep asking for.
- **Builders' merchants.** Cement, rebar, sections, tube and some steel fixings are on the list; check each line by commodity code, because plenty of what a merchant sells is not. On default values and illustrative rates, chargeable Portland cement carries about £12 a tonne (roughly 30p on a 25kg bag) and ribbed rebar about £29 a tonne. Goods count when they arrive, not when they were ordered. Bringing them from Ireland can reach the £50,000 registration test quickly, and a firm that expects to reach it within 30 days is registrable from the first load, and until regulations are made Irish cement counts towards it. EU-origin lines should then drop out; Turkish rebar bought through an Irish wholesaler will not.
- **Farms and merchants.** Farming is in neither ETS and food is not a CBAM good. Fertiliser is. At HMRC's illustrative rate the charge on chargeable CAN is about £3 a tonne and on urea about £2, paid by the importer. It will rise as free allowances for producers are withdrawn. A farmer who buys fertiliser, or a steel-framed shed, direct from across the border can be the importer. Nothing in the deal puts a carbon price on imported food, so the farming unions' leakage argument is untouched. The separate food and farm-goods (SPS) agreement, aimed at 1 March 2028, would ease the food paperwork GB firms now face on sales into the EU, narrowing an advantage NI has today.
- **Food and drink processors.** Some empty steel and aluminium packaging, such as cans, drums and foil, is on the CBAM list; check the commodity code, and note that the charge falls on whoever imports it, which is usually your supplier. Sites with a full UK ETS permit should plan on the linked price and on free allocations moving to EU benchmarks from 2028. Small-emitter and ultra-small-emitter statuses already granted are protected to 2030.
- **GB exporters.** Your EU importers owe the EU CBAM on 2026 and 2027 goods, unless they bring in under 50 tonnes a year in total. If you sell delivered duty paid, the liability can come back to you through your EU customs representative. Keep supplying the data.
- **Hauliers and anyone paying Irish Sea freight.** Ships of 5,000 gross tonnage and above have been in the UK ETS since 1 July 2026. Under the linking terms the temporary 50% reduction for voyages between NI and GB is removed, so operators' carbon costs on those crossings rise, at the linked price. There is no official costing yet; expect it in surcharges. Road fuel is not in either ETS and the deal does not change that. A carrier is not the importer for CBAM unless it makes the customs declaration in its own name.

## Electricity: the EU charges it, the UK does not

Electricity is on the EU's CBAM list and not on the UK's. Power sent from Great Britain to Northern Ireland is not affected: the government's position is that the EU CBAM does not apply here ([written answer HL12132](https://questions-statements.parliament.uk/written-questions/detail/2025-11-19/hl12132), 3 December 2025). Power moves between NI and Ireland inside the all-island market with no border charge, which is why NI's power stations stay in the EU ETS. Power sent from Great Britain to Ireland is an import into the EU and has been inside the EU CBAM since January 2026. NI wholesale prices are set in the all-island market, so any cost on that flow reaches bills here too. The summit papers do not deal with electricity separately; as a listed good it should fall within the EU's expected 2028 exemption, but that is not confirmed. Separate talks on electricity trading are due to conclude in 2027.

## Biomethane: not unlocked yet

Northern Ireland is consulting on a biomethane strategy until 15 December 2026, and many gas-using manufacturers are being offered certified "green gas". The deal does not yet make it count. The government's explainer says the UK ETS will go on recognising biomethane that a site produces and uses directly, but biomethane injected into the gas grid cannot yet generate claimed reductions. That waits for a system to prevent double counting, introduced in accordance with EU law. From 2028 even gas made on site needs evidence that it meets the EU's sustainability criteria. The EU ETS already has one, with conditions: sustainability evidence, no double counting, and buyer and producer on the same gas grid. From 2028 the UK is to follow those EU monitoring rules, so the route is visible, but no date is set.

Biomethane is not itself a CBAM good. It matters as the fuel behind one: the EU CBAM counts biogas as zero only with sustainability evidence, and HMRC's draft guidance for the UK CBAM is silent on gas taken from the grid. So a site in the UK ETS should not pay a premium for certified grid biomethane expecting a lower carbon bill until the rule exists. Stormont's proposal is a limited support scheme for biomethane used in industrial heat, aimed at 2028 to 2035: a preferred position, not an approved scheme. For digester operators, today's income is the renewables obligation for electricity; income from biomethane depends on that scheme. A farmer asked to supply slurry should ask who takes the digestate back, and on what terms.

## The case for and against

**For.** A larger, more liquid carbon market. No border charge or data burden on UK–EU trade once both exemptions apply. For NI, the oddity of a UK tax on goods from across the border should go, and NI power and NI industry face the same carbon price.

**Against.** The UK follows EU carbon-market rules without a vote and gives up its own price floor. UK sites in the ETS are likely to pay roughly £9 to £11 a tonne more at today's prices, depending on the exchange rate. NI industry pays more for carbon with no matching fall in power costs, because NI generators already pay the EU price. And NI loses its niche as the one part of the UK outside the EU CBAM.

It is also contested. Labour would keep the UK ETS and CBAM. The Conservatives and Reform UK have pledged to abolish them (press reports). Ending the UK ETS would lift a cost from every UK site, and would also put GB-made goods back into the EU's border charge. Firms should plan for either outcome.

## It will not fix Northern Ireland's carbon budget

Agriculture is 31.4% of NI emissions and 10% above its 1990 level. Transport is 21.3% and buildings 15.5%. Apart from larger ships since July 2026, none is in an ETS. Power and industry, the sectors the deal touches, are about a sixth. The 2028–32 budget asks for an average cut of 48%, and the Climate Action Plan is still not laid. This agreement does not change that sum.

## What to do now

1. List every supplier of steel, aluminium, cement or fertiliser and mark each as GB, Irish or other.
2. Ask each one, for every invoice line, the 8-digit commodity code and the country of origin under the non-preferential origin rules. For steel, ask for the country of melt and pour as well; it is a different test and the two can differ.
3. If these goods cross the border from Ireland to you, add up what you expect to buy that way in 2027, at customs value. The test is £50,000 over the past 12 months, counting only from 1 January 2027, or expected in the next 30 days. Once you pass it, register with HMRC by 31 January 2028; registration opens on 1 January 2028 and an agent cannot do it for you. Goods imported before you passed the test are not charged (Finance Act 2026, section 147).
4. Keep the records from 1 January 2027: code, date, value, weight, origin and evidence for any exemption. HMRC requires them of anyone importing these goods, liable or not, for six years, and there are penalties for failing to register, file, pay or keep records.
5. Treat the exemption for EU-origin goods as likely, not as law. HMRC's policy summary, last updated on 2 November before the summit, still said when read on 20 November that no country is exempt.
6. If you plan to use default values, know that a return made on defaults cannot later be amended with actual emissions data.
