224 responses to the draft Climate Action Plan. 100 to the Just Transition Commission. 59 to the adaptation programme. 67 to the Peatland Strategy. My response to the Department for Infrastructure opens by asking why returns like these are received so calmly by officials and elected members, and whether that is by design or by default. Then it turns to the substance.

I support the Department's redesign of the classes and thresholds that decide whether a planning application is local, major or regionally significant. But I ask it to go further in the direction it has chosen. In Northern Ireland a major application means a 12-week community consultation and then, on the Department's own 2025/26 figures, an average 41.4 weeks to a decision against a 30-week target. So every threshold is a year of someone's project. My response applies one test to each proposal: does the extra process change the decision, or only delay it?

## What DfI asked

The Planning (Development Management) Regulations 2015 sort development into nine classes and set the thresholds at which a proposal becomes major development, with pre-application community consultation, and at which the Department must be consulted on whether it is regionally significant. DfI proposes to rebuild the energy class around generation, storage, and transmission and distribution; to raise the generating-station threshold from 5 MW to 10 MW; to add energy storage thresholds in megawatt-hours; to make every hazardous waste and hydrocarbon proposal major; to add geothermal drilling and carbon capture; and to tidy the transport, extractive and catch-all classes. Housing stays at 50 units. Northern Ireland received 9,642 applications in 2025/26, of which 165 were major and four regionally significant; the review is about the boundary around those 169.

## Five positions behind the response

All of them sit under one test: the transition must not impoverish the people asked to pay for it, here in Northern Ireland or anywhere in the UK, Ireland, the EU or the West. A policy that raises bills, exports jobs and imports its equipment from coal-fired factories is a transfer of wealth, not a transition.

- **Keep small modular reactors open.** Nuclear is already Class 1; say so in the energy class, and agree a Northern Ireland siting position with DfE now that the UK's EN-7 policy statement covers SMRs with criteria-based siting.
- **Storage is the cure for wasted wind.** Northern Ireland dispatched down nearly 30% of its available wind in 2024 and 22% in 2025, most of it network constraint. A Schedule that makes a battery beside a constrained wind farm a year-long process is working against the grid.
- **Demand reduction first.** A voucher-based insulation and efficiency scheme for every household and small business, with the training to deliver it, beats any generation class. Planning's job is to stay out of the way of retrofit, small heat networks and building-scale storage.
- **Don't offshore production.** Energy-intensive manufacturers face the UK CBAM from January. Carbon pricing without a quick route to on-site generation, storage, heat recovery and capture moves emissions and jobs rather than cutting them.
- **Bring the equipment inside the carbon border, and judge it on the whole of ESG.** China makes about 80% of the world's solar modules and batteries and over 60% of its wind turbines, on coal power and, in Xinjiang, with forced labour; Congolese cobalt is dug by children; Brazilian pig iron is smelted on charcoal from slave-labour camps in the Amazon. Neither CBAM yet covers the finished product. The EU is extending its CBAM to downstream goods from 2028 and may include turbines and panels; the UK should match it. Until then, every major scheme should declare what its components cost in carbon, labour and dependence, and the premium that clean, forced-labour-free kit carries should be borne through support schemes and procurement, where fuel-poor households can be shielded from it, not through the regulated tariff.

## The eighteen asks

1. **20 MW for generation, as Scotland has had since 2009,** with a 10 ha site-area criterion for ground-mounted solar. At 10 MW Northern Ireland would still have the lowest threshold in these islands; England raised solar to 100 MW last December.
2. **Say how repowering and co-located storage are classified.** Regulation 2(1)(b) already judges an extension by the addition alone; say the same for repowering within a permitted boundary and for batteries added to a consented site.
3. **A "fuel production" description in Class 2,** so electrolysers and biomethane plants are classified by capacity, not floorspace.
4. **COMAH, not gigawatt-hours, for hydrogen and ammonia storage.** A 1 GWh hydrogen store is about 30 tonnes, six times the lower-tier quantity; a 5-tonne store would be local. Operators already do the COMAH sum; the Schedule should use it.
5. **Don't make batteries major on safety grounds; make the fire service a statutory consultee.** England took storage out of its national regime in 2020 because the planning impacts are low. Finish the definition of "electricity storage" first.
6. **A de minimis for hazardous waste,** so a council recycling centre adding an asbestos skip is not a 12-month process.
7. **Say where anaerobic digestion sits** (waste feedstock: Class 4; crops and manures: Class 2).
8. **Widen carbon capture** to capture plant at process sites above 100,000 t/yr; Northern Ireland has no underground storage but will have capture, driven by the UK CBAM from January.
9. **Keep closed-loop ground-source arrays out of the 200 m geothermal threshold.**
10. **Peat is the one place I ask for more scrutiny:** 150 ha for regional significance is too high against the Peatland Strategy to 2040.
11. **A prescribed 28-day period for the Department's section 26 notice,** with performance published, and an amendment to the Act so that silence becomes a notice. New "all proposals" categories will send more cases up; Scotland manages without a referral step at all.
12. **Publish the numbers.** The Regulatory Impact Assessment has none. Applications by class since 2015, how many would move under each threshold, and a review in 2028 rather than 2030.
13. **Name nuclear generation, including SMRs, in Class 2** (nuclear is left out of Class 1), and prepare a siting position alongside EN-7.
14. **Classify energy infrastructure inside an existing industrial site by the addition,** on the same thresholds as stand-alone schemes, and recognise carbon leakage as a consideration when such applications are determined.
15. **An embodied-carbon and supply-chain statement for major energy development,** covering carbon, forced and child labour and local pollution; embodied carbon counts where it bears on a scheme's claimed climate benefit, the rest informs support schemes; and pressure through the Executive for the UK CBAM to cover finished energy equipment on the EU's 2028 timetable, with origin-neutral conditions on NI support schemes.
16. **Set each threshold against the EU and GB rules Northern Ireland already carries and cannot shape.** Around 300 EU acts apply here under the Windsor Framework, scrutiny comes after agreement, not before, and the planning hierarchy is one of the few levers wholly in Stormont's gift. Use EU-derived definitions where NI must apply them anyway, so it doesn't end up with the strictest classification and the least say.
17. **Name interconnectors in Class 2 and east–west connectivity in Class 3.** NI is the only part of the island inside NATO, and EU Connecting Europe money funds links to France, not Great Britain, so strategies have followed the money. The all-island market that followed the Good Friday Agreement, kept inside EU rules by the Windsor Framework, is small, gas-dependent and thinly linked to Great Britain: Europe-high wholesale prices, the Republic's household electricity now the dearest in the EU, one power cable from NI to Scotland, two from the Republic to Wales and one to France from 2028. Energy independence means firm local low-carbon generation and storage, plus strong links east–west in power, freight, rail and cables; the Schedule should make those the easiest things to consent.
18. **Publish who responded and how responses were weighed.** NI consultations draw responses in the tens to low hundreds (Climate Action Plan 224, Just Transition Commission 100, adaptation programme 59); a cohort that small is not directional and is open to being skewed by campaign or abusive submissions. Weigh evidence, not volume, and publish the rules in advance.

I also support the switch to annual tonnage for non-hazardous waste (the same 25,000 t/yr as Scotland), the "area of works" definition that captures temporary spoil heaps, and treating all onshore works for offshore wind as major. The full response answers each of DfI's seven questions, and adds points on rural impact, contaminated land, definitions, EIA alignment and transition.

## Why it matters to clients

The threshold decides whether a battery, a heat network, a hydrogen store, an AD plant or a capture unit goes through twelve weeks of community consultation before an application is lodged, and whether DfI rather than the council decides it. For manufacturers investing to meet the UK CBAM from January 2027, that is time and cost that should be predictable and proportionate.
