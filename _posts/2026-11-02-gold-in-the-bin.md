*Part of the [Policy series]({{ '/policy-series/' | relative_url }}): where the rules stand in Northern Ireland, who decides them, and what to do about it. Updated as the rules change.*

## The argument in brief

On 1 October 2026 the Irish News reported that three large fires since 2021 at a Belfast harbour scrapyard had cost the Fire and Rescue Service £171,887. The service named a lithium battery as the probable cause of the latest, in September. The same yard appears on DAERA's register as one of Northern Ireland's three approved WEEE exporters. Three months earlier, on 8 July 2026, a Commons committee approved the rules that let EU battery law be enforced in Northern Ireland. The DUP and TUV opposed them, on cost and on sovereignty.

Together, those two stories show the problem. Electrical waste holds real value: gold, palladium, copper, cobalt, lithium and rare earths. It also holds real hazards, from lithium cells to refrigerant gases. The paperwork meant to follow it from producer to refiner breaks where the value is highest. Northern Ireland sits under two rulebooks. The EU Batteries Regulation applies here under the Windsor Framework, while the UK WEEE Regulations also apply. Reform in GB has stalled.

The argument of this piece is that the gap between rules on paper and material in practice is now a bigger risk than any one regulation. Firms that can show where their waste ended up will be better placed than firms holding only a certificate.

*This piece sets out the arguments on each side; the recommendations are the author's. Facts checked to 30 October 2026.*

## The lifecycle at a glance

Material leaks out between collection and treatment, where paperwork is thinnest. Critical elements are lost again at the smelter unless they were separated first.

The weak link is stage 5 to stage 9. An AATF evidence note shows that waste was treated. It does not show what was finally recovered, or where.

## Why some elements come back and others do not

A chemist's note. Copper smelters run hot and oxidising. Gold, silver and palladium are noble metals: they resist oxidation and dissolve into molten copper, which acts as a collector. Electrorefining then drops them into anode slimes, where they are recovered.

Rare earths, tantalum, gallium, lithium and aluminium behave differently. They bind to oxygen very strongly, so they form stable oxides that pass into the slag. There they are too dilute to recover at a profit. Some gallium, germanium and indium also escape as fume into flue dust.

The practical point follows from the chemistry. To recover the critical elements, they must be separated before the furnace: by dismantling, by removing magnets, or by battery-specific processing. A trail that ends with "sent to smelter" has already lost them.

- **Alignment against sovereignty.** At Westminster the battery debate was about whether EU law should be enforced in NI, not about whether batteries need controls.

- **Producer pays or council pays.** The LGA wants producers to fund the fridge backlog (1 Sep 2026). Producer-funded kerbside collection is still undecided.

- **Who regulates.** The Conservative plan to abolish the Environment Agency would change who approves treatment sites in England.

- **Silence.** Few parties have an e-waste policy, which leaves the field to officials and industry.

## Who decides what

Most rules are set in London or Brussels. NI's position depends on whether the EU instrument appears in Annex 2 of the Windsor Framework.

## Where the pillars compete

- **Export bans against livelihoods (E against S).** WHO estimates up to 18 million children may be at risk from informal e-waste recycling (15 Jun 2021). The Basel tightening protects them, but it can also cut income from repair markets that rely on used imports.

- **Urban mining against primary mining (E against S).** Recycled cobalt and nickel reduce demand for mined ore, and so the social risks of new mines. Yet recycling meets only about 1% of global rare-earth demand (UN Global E-waste Monitor, 20 Mar 2024).

- **Smelting against mining (E against E).** Smelters burn plastics and need fossil-heavy heat. The balance usually favours recovery for precious metals, but furnace emissions fall on the communities near them.

- **Data security against reuse (S against E).** Firms shred laptops to protect data. That destroys reuse, the most valuable stage.

- **Fire safety against convenience (S against E).** Vapes were linked to 23% of reported battery fires (Circular Online, 15 Jul 2026). Banning their sale cut litter, but take-back still depends on retailers.

## What it means for Northern Ireland firms and councils

- Battery suppliers into NI now face OPSS enforcement, NI labelling and, from 18 Feb 2027, a passport for EV, light-transport and large industrial batteries. GB suppliers do not yet face the same.

- Digital waste tracking becomes mandatory for NI receivers in January 2027 (DAERA), three months after England and Wales. Expect paper notes to run alongside it at first.

- NI has nine AATFs and no metal refinery. Circuit boards, black mass and magnets leave NI for GB or the EU, and shipments to Ireland follow EU rules.

- Councils carry fire risk, fridge storage and collection costs with no settled producer-funding model.

- Manufacturers asked about recycled content, by customers or under ecodesign rules, will need evidence at refiner level, not just an AATF certificate.

- A business is responsible for its own end-of-life equipment. Data-bearing devices need a sanitisation route that allows reuse.

## Practical examples

- **A battery fire at a Belfast WEEE exporter.** The fire service said a lithium battery was the probable cause of the September 2026 fire at the Clearway Disposals yard, which cost £75,455 to fight. NIEA said it is considering extra lithium-ion requirements for waste operators ([Irish News, 1 Oct 2026](https://www.irishnews.com/news/northern-ireland/repeated-fires-at-belfast-scrapyard-cost-fire-and-rescue-service-significant-sum-4WSQIQOTURAKRLRI2AWESFX7QY/)).

- **Battery enforcement now live in NI.** SI 2026/1053 switches off the GB-derived 2008 battery rules in NI and adds penalties for breaching the EU Batteries Regulation. Passports follow for EV, light-transport and large industrial batteries from 18 Feb 2027 ([OPSS guidance, 17 Oct 2026](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/eu-batteries-regulations-in-northern-ireland)).

- **Who can take NI's e-waste.** DAERA lists nine AATF approvals held by seven operators, and three approved exporters, in Belfast, Toomebridge and Lisburn ([DAERA, updated 21 Jan 2026](https://www.daera-ni.gov.uk/articles/waste-electrical-and-electronic-equipment-weee-treatment-facilities)). A firm's take-back contract will lead to one of these.

- **Digital waste tracking.** The receipt service opened on 28 Apr 2026, voluntary at first. The Environment Agency puts the cost of waste crime at £1bn a year ([EA blog, 30 Apr 2026](https://environmentagency.blog.gov.uk/2026/04/30/digital-waste-tracking-goes-live-a-major-step-forward-in-stopping-waste-crime/)). NI receivers must use it from January 2027 ([GOV.UK, updated 1 Oct 2026](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/digital-waste-tracking-service/digital-waste-tracking-service)).

- **The fridge backlog.** The LGA found dozens of English councils had stopped or restricted fridge and freezer collections after a reprocessor closed, fires and summer heat. It asked producers to pay the extra storage costs ([letsrecycle, 1 Sep 2026](https://www.letsrecycle.com/news/lga-calls-for-producers-to-pick-up-costs-for-fridge-capacity-issues/)).

- **Workplace small WEEE in Wales.** Welsh workplaces must present small WEEE separately for collection from 6 Apr 2026, subject to Senedd approval of the regulations ([Welsh Government, 27 Jan 2026](https://www.gov.wales/written-statement-laying-waste-separation-requirements-wales-amendment-regulations-2026)). It shows how a business duty can be added without waiting for UK reform.

## Recommendations

1.  **Defra and DAERA: publish the decision on household collection and retailer duties.** It was promised for 2025, and councils need funding certainty.

2.  **DAERA: give NI waste receivers a readiness timetable before January 2027.** An Environment Agency blog of 30 Apr 2026 gave October 2026 for NI, so some sites may have planned for the wrong date.

3.  **Defra: run the promised UK-wide batteries consultation this autumn.** Aligning GB with NI would end the two-tier market the DUP objected to.

4.  **NIEA: publish NI WEEE tonnages and export volumes by stream each year.** No public NI breakdown could be found.

5.  **UK Government: tie Vision 2035 funding to UK pre-processing of magnets and black mass.** These materials are lost if they go to a smelter first.

6.  **Firms: ask for downstream mass-balance evidence to refiner level.** An evidence note proves treatment, not recovery.

7.  **Councils: collect batteries and vapes separately at kerbside and recycling centres, and log every fire.** Fire data shapes insurance terms and producer funding claims.

8.  **Firms: choose certified data-wiping over shredding for IT.** Reuse keeps value and cuts emissions.
