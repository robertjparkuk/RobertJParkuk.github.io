Northern Ireland has no deer census, no cull return and, as far as I can confirm, no approved game-handling plant. Yet its venison is lean, wild and in demand, and most of the venison the UK eats is imported. This piece follows game from the hill to the plate and on to the skins and feathers. It covers who may shoot, sell and process it, what changes for lead shot and cured meat, and why NI sits under EU food law while GB does not. It weighs venison's carbon case, including the deer's own methane, and the part deer control plays in NI's tree-planting plan. It also sets out the case against released-bird shooting.

*General information as at 21 November 2026, not legal advice. Claims I have not checked against a primary source are in italics.* The full briefing, with every section, table and source, is a 48-page PDF: [download the full briefing (PDF)]({{ '/assets/game/2026-11/whos-a-pheasant-plucker-game-meat-ni.pdf' | relative_url }}). This page is the accessible version.

## Key findings

- **Nobody counts NI's deer.** There is no census and no cull return, so every claim about numbers rests on all-island studies, estates and trade bodies.
- **Selling game is simple; selling wild duck is not.** Game and game-dealer licences were abolished in 2011. Selling a dead wild bird other than woodpigeon, mallard included, is an offence under the 1985 Order.
- **NI sits under EU food law for game.** Game from an approved NI establishment can go to GB and the EU; game coming in from GB needs certificates.
- **Lead goes later here.** GB bans the sale and use of lead shot, and large-calibre lead bullets for live quarry, from 1 April 2029. The EU ban on lead gunshot, which applies in NI because it amends EU REACH, starts on 7 December 2033 and does not cover bullets.
- **Venison's methane is real but sits outside NI's carbon budget.** On an old UK farmed-deer factor, an adult deer emits about 288 kg CO2e a year. *Wild deer are believed to fall outside the national inventory.*
- **Deer control is part of NI's tree-planting plan, though the plan does not say so.** Planting has to rise from about 600 ha a year to more than 2,000 ha from 2032.
- **Processing may be the real bottleneck.** *No currently approved NI game-handling establishment has been confirmed*, and skins and feathers are mostly not used.
- **Curing is the obvious route to value**, under the lower EU nitrite limits that have applied in NI since 9 October 2025.

## Where game comes from

Wild deer (fallow, sika and red), released pheasant and red-legged partridge, wildfowl, woodcock, snipe, red grouse, rabbit, woodpigeon and the Irish hare make up NI's game. Supply comes from estate stalking, Forest Service culls, private and syndicate shoots, and wildfowling clubs. No agency maps game in NI as a whole. A Commons written answer in 2003 said no statutory body routinely collects overall wild deer numbers in Northern Ireland ([Hansard, 22 January 2003](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/written-answers/2003/jan/22/deer)), and I have found nothing since that changes that.

Seasons are set in law: deer by [Schedule 10 of the Wildlife (NI) Order 1985](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/nisi/1985/171/schedule/10), game birds and hares by [section 7 of the Game Preservation Act (NI) 1928](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/apni/1928/25/section/7). Deer must be shot with a rifle of at least .236 inch (6 mm), with a muzzle energy of at least 1,700 foot-pounds (2,305 joules) and a bullet of at least 100 grains or an expanding bullet ([Schedule 11](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/nisi/1985/171/schedule/11)); best practice is both. Muntjac and Chinese water deer may be shot with a smaller rifle ([Article 20(8A)](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/nisi/1985/171/article/20)).

## Who may sell it

NI abolished game licences and game-dealer licences from 13 June 2011 ([SR 2011/215](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/nisr/2011/215/made)). From 17 August 2011 the ban on selling game out of season ended, selling game known to have been taken unlawfully became an offence, and the venison dealer's licence went ([2011 Act, s36](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/nia/2011/15/section/36); [SR 2011/285](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/nisr/2011/285/article/2/made)).

Wild birds are different. Under [Article 7 of the 1985 Order](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/nisi/1985/171/article/7), selling a dead wild bird is an offence unless it is listed in Schedule 3, and woodpigeon is the only one listed. Mallard, teal and geese can be shot in season but not sold. Pheasant, partridge, woodcock, snipe and red grouse are "game-birds" under [Article 2](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/nisi/1985/171/article/2), not "wild birds", so they can be sold.

Food law then decides how. A hunter can supply small quantities of game direct to the final consumer or a local shop under the local-supply route, with council registration. Anything wider needs an approved game-handling establishment, approved in NI by the FSA ([FSA NI approvals policy, March 2025](https://www.food.gov.uk/print/pdf/node/10816); [FSA wild game guidance](https://food.gov.uk/business-guidance/wild-game-guidance)). *I have not been able to confirm that any NI game-handling establishment is currently approved.* If none is, most NI game for wider sale has to cross the Irish Sea to be processed, and comes back as meat that needs a certificate.

## Markets: short of venison, long on pheasant

Game has two markets that behave in opposite ways. Venison is short of home supply: most UK venison imports come from New Zealand. *The Scottish Venison Association put UK imports at about 3,000 tonnes a year (trade-body estimate, not official).* In GB, game birds are oversupplied, and some dealers now charge shoots to collect them. *NI's position is not known.*

The Windsor Framework splits the picture. NI follows EU food hygiene law for game, so game from an approved NI establishment can move to GB and the EU, while wild game coming into NI from GB needs an export health certificate ([GOV.UK, certificate 8372](https://www.gov.uk/export-health-certificates/wild-game-bird-meat-to-the-european-union-and-northern-ireland-certificate-8372)). The UK–EU SPS agreement concluded on 20 November 2026 is meant to apply from 1 March 2028; *what its final text means for wild game has not been checked.*

## Carbon: methane, woodland and peat

Wild venison's carbon case depends on one accounting choice: whether you count the deer's own methane. A 2009 life-cycle study for the Deer Commission for Scotland put wild venison's footprint 38% below beef and 49% below lamb, with methane from digestion and dung making up 76% of the total ([NatureScot Archive Report 024](https://www.nature.scot/doc/naturescot-archive-report-024-life-cycle-assessment-scottish-wild-venison)).

The UK inventory once carried a factor for farmed deer: 10.4 kg of methane a head a year from digestion and 0.26 kg from manure for adults ([NAEI annual report 1998, Table A40](https://uk-air.defra.gov.uk/assets/documents/reports/empire/naei/annreport/annrep98/app1_212.html)). At the IPCC's 100-year factor for non-fossil methane, 27.0, that is about 288 kg CO2e per adult deer a year. Methane lasts about 12 years in the air, not centuries, which is why some argue for other metrics; I use GWP100 because UK and EU reporting does. *Wild deer are believed to sit outside the UK and NI inventory totals; this has not been confirmed in the inventory documentation.* If so, a cull cuts real emissions without moving NI's official figures.

The bigger carbon question is trees. Woodland covers 8.6% of NI, about 119,000 ha (forestry statistics at 31 March 2025). DAERA's first [Tree Planting Action Plan 2026–2032](https://www.daera-ni.gov.uk/publications/tree-planting-action-plan-2026-2032) says planting must rise from about 600 ha a year to more than 2,000 ha a year from 2032. It supports the aim of 12% woodland cover by 2050 ([DAERA, 7 July 2026](https://www.daera-ni.gov.uk/news/muir-publishes-northern-irelands-first-tree-planting-action-plan)). For 2023 to 2027 the target is 2,585 ha; 1,386 ha had been planted, which the plan calls 57% (the figures give 54%). The plan does not mention deer.

Young trees need protecting from browsing. Forest Research found deer activity limits tree recruitment on woodland-creation sites ([29 May 2026](https://www.forestresearch.gov.uk/publications/deer-activity-limits-tree-recruitment-in-woodland-creation-sites/)). Fencing is costly at scale; stalking is the other tool. That makes deer control part of NI's carbon plan, and venison a by-product of it. The plan keeps a presumption against planting on deep peat and active raised bog, and says forest soils hold two to three times more carbon than the trees above them. The carbon case lies in the management, not in the meat.

## Health, lead and cooking

Venison is lean and high in protein and iron. The main health question is lead. The FSA advises frequent eaters of lead-shot game, especially toddlers, children, pregnant women and women trying for a baby, to cut down ([FSA](https://www.food.gov.uk/science/advice-to-frequent-eaters-of-game-shot-with-lead)). Cut away meat around the wound channel and ask the supplier whether lead was used. Eating raw deer liver carries a real risk of hepatitis E ([Tei et al., Lancet 2003](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12907011/)).

An AFBI-led study found *Mycobacterium bovis*, the cause of bovine TB, in 13 of 522 deer culled across NI in 2019 to 2023, about one in forty ([Allen et al., 2025](https://experts.arizona.edu/en/publications/assessing-the-potential-role-of-deer-in-the-dissemination-of-myco/)). Trained hunters check carcases in the field for exactly this kind of sign.

## Processing and curing

Large wild game must be chilled to no more than 7°C throughout, small game to 4°C ([FSA wild game guidance](https://food.gov.uk/business-guidance/wild-game-guidance)). Curing adds value, and here NI and GB now differ. [Commission Regulation (EU) 2023/2108](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2023/2108/oj/eng) cut the maximum added nitrite in meat products from 9 October 2025, and the FSA confirmed it applies in NI ([FSA explanatory memorandum](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6577273f254aaa0010050a52/EM_for_EU_legislation_applying_to_NI_-_Nitrates__FSAWNI__FSS__C_23_6632.pdf)). An NI curer selling into GB and the EU should make to the EU limits.

Skins, antler and feathers are Category 3 animal by-products. Most deer skins in NI are not used. *There is no published NI figure.*

## Road kill, in short

Road kill can never be sold or supplied as food. No NI offence has been found in taking a road-killed pheasant or rabbit for your own household, but nothing official says it is safe to eat. Leave dead deer where they lie: removing a deer carcase from someone's land without consent can be poaching ([Article 22](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/nisi/1985/171/article/22)). Wild ducks, geese, birds of prey and badgers are protected even when dead.

## The case against

The case against released-bird shooting is real. Pheasant and red-legged partridge are non-native, released in numbers that change the local ecology, and some shot birds go uneaten. Predator control on shoots kills crows and foxes; conservation managers answer that it helps ground-nesting birds, and at Glenwherry red grouse and Irish hare counts rose under predator control and habitat work. Lead shot can poison scavenging birds. The female deer season opens on 1 November, when calves still depend on their mothers, and runs to 31 March, when hinds and does are heavily pregnant; stalkers answer that shooting the calf first keeps orphaning down. *No NI data exists either way.*

My view: the venison case stands or falls on the cull being necessary and the meat being used, not on the shooting of released birds.

## What would help

1. **A simple NI deer return** (species, sex, location), run by DAERA alongside the existing seasons. A voluntary return through BASC NI and the estates could come first.
2. **An approved NI game-handling establishment**, or a confirmed list of the ones that exist, so NI venison can be processed here.
3. **Public-sector venison** from Forest Service culls for hospital, school and prison catering, through an approved establishment.
4. **Curing to EU limits**, so NI products can sell into both markets.
5. **Deer control written into the tree-planting plan** as a running cost, not a one-off.

## Questions people ask

**Do I need a licence to sell game in Northern Ireland?** No. Game and game-dealer licences were abolished in 2011. Selling game known to have been taken unlawfully is an offence. Supplying game meat locally needs council registration; processing for wider sale needs FSA approval. Selling dead wild ducks and other wild birds, apart from woodpigeon, is an offence.

**Can I pick up and eat road kill in NI?** No NI offence has been found in taking a road-killed pheasant or rabbit for your own household, but nothing official says it is safe to eat, and road kill can never be sold. Leave dead deer, wild ducks, geese, birds of prey and badgers where they lie.

**When do the lead shot rules change in NI?** Using lead shot on or over wetland is already an offence. GB bans the sale and use of lead shot from 1 April 2029. The EU ban on lead gunshot, which applies in NI, starts on 7 December 2033 and does not cover bullets.

**Is venison low-carbon?** It depends on whether the deer's own methane is counted. In a 2009 Scottish study methane made up 76% of the footprint, which still came out 38% below beef. Where a cull is needed anyway, the extra carbon from eating the meat is mostly fuel, chilling and processing.

**Is it safe to eat game shot with lead?** The FSA advises frequent eaters to cut down, especially children and pregnant women. Cut away meat around the wound and ask whether lead was used.
