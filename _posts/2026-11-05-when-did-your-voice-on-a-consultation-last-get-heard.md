*Northern Ireland's rules are written in two places, and most of the people who live under them never take part in either. That isn't apathy. It's a system that asks for your views and rarely tells you what it did with them.*

<figure class="page-photo"><img src="{{ '/assets/photos/boardroom.jpg' | relative_url }}" alt="An empty boardroom table with chairs" loading="lazy"><figcaption>The decisions get made either way. The question is who is in the room.</figcaption></figure>

Here's a test. Think of the last Stormont consultation you responded to. Now think of when you found out what happened to your response. If you can't answer the second question, you're in good company.

DAERA's draft Climate Action Plan, which sets out how every sector of the economy will cut its emissions to 2027, drew **224 responses** when it was consulted on in 2025. The consultation on the Just Transition Commission drew **100**. The draft third climate adaptation programme drew **59**. These are plans that will shape investment decisions, farm incomes, council budgets and household bills for years. A few hundred people and organisations had their say.

On the EU side, the picture is starker. Around 300 EU laws apply in Northern Ireland under the Windsor Framework, covering goods, customs, food, chemicals, product safety and the electricity market. Northern Ireland has no vote on them, and was never meant to: the settlement gives it a voice, not a vote. But a voice only counts if it is used and heard.

## How the voice works now

**At Stormont.** Departments usually consult for 12 weeks, the norm in Section 75 equality schemes, and many follow the UK Government's 2018 Consultation Principles. Those principles ask departments to publish their response within 12 weeks of a consultation closing, or explain why not. In practice:

- The Climate Change Act (Northern Ireland) 2022 set deadlines for the first Climate Action Plan at the end of 2023 and June 2024. In June 2025 the Minister told the Assembly both had been missed. The summary of consultation responses came out in April 2026. In September, the plan was still with the Executive for approval.
- The carbon budgets consultation closed in October 2023. Its summary of responses followed about seven months later.
- A discussion paper on a Clean Air Strategy came out in November 2020. The draft strategy was out for consultation until 27 October this year, nearly six years later.
- The Just Transition Commission was consulted on in winter 2024–25. As of early 2026, it had still not been set up.

I'm not suggesting bad faith. Officials work hard on these, often with thin teams and an Executive that didn't sit for two of the last four years. But the loop between "tell us what you think" and "here's what we did" is long, and nothing enforces it.

**At the Assembly.** The Windsor Framework Democratic Scrutiny Committee first met in February 2024. It has published 15 inquiry reports so far, on subjects from toy safety to the Union Customs Code. But it has five working days to decide whether to hold an inquiry on a new EU act, and the legal test for action is high: a significant, lasting impact on everyday life. In its first session it found seven acts significantly different from what they replaced, and none that met the test. The Stormont Brake has been pulled once, in December 2024, on chemical labelling rules. The Secretary of State declined to act.

**In Brussels.** Anyone can respond to a European Commission consultation through its *Have Your Say* portal, including NI businesses. Most people here don't know that. Even when they do, the feedback is slow: the European Court of Auditors found in 2019 that the Commission's summaries of consultation responses took nine months on average, and only a third of respondents believed their views were taken into account.

**In between.** Lord Murphy's independent review of the Windsor Framework received over 500 submissions. He found smaller firms bewildered by guidance that isn't in plain English, and recommended more time and staff for the Assembly's scrutiny committee and wider stakeholder access to UK–EU bodies. The House of Lords' Northern Ireland Scrutiny Committee said in October 2025 that efforts to close the democratic deficit were insufficient, and noted there is no central register of the EU laws that apply here. The Government accepted Lord Murphy's recommendations in December 2025, including a £16.6 million one-stop shop for business guidance.

## Three scenarios

These are illustrative scenarios from my work in public affairs, not forecasts.

**1. Silence.** Most businesses and community groups keep out. Consultations are answered by the same few dozen organisations: the big trade bodies, the well-funded campaigns, the people paid to respond. Policy is shaped by whoever turns up. Six months later, a small manufacturer finds a new rule it never saw coming, and concludes that consultations are a waste of time. The cycle repeats.

**2. Noise.** A campaign drives thousands of identical responses. Officials count them, file them, and give them the weight that a copied form deserves. The volume rises, the influence doesn't, and trust in the process falls further.

**3. Signal.** More businesses, councils and community groups respond, briefly and with evidence: what the proposal will cost them, what it will change, what would work better. Departments publish what they heard and what they changed within 12 weeks. The Assembly's scrutiny committee gets the time and evidence to look properly at the EU acts that matter. This is the scenario where Northern Ireland's voice starts to count.

## Six fixes

For government:

1. **A 12-week duty to close the loop.** Every Stormont department should publish its response to a consultation within 12 weeks of closing, or publish the reason it can't, and say what changed as a result. The UK principles already set that standard. Make it the rule here.
2. **A public consultation tracker.** One page listing every NI consultation, its closing date, the number of responses and the date of the outcome. Nobody publishes the overall numbers today. Until someone does, I publish my own responses, with closing dates and outcomes, on my [Consultation responses]({{ '/public-sector/consultation-responses/' | relative_url }}) page.
3. **More time and evidence for scrutiny.** Give the Democratic Scrutiny Committee 10 working days, as Lord Murphy recommended, and publish an early-warning list of upcoming EU proposals so businesses can respond in Brussels before the law is settled, not after.
4. **One front door for EU law in NI.** A single, plain-English register of the EU laws that apply here, as the Lords committee asked. My [EU Law and the Windsor Framework dashboard]({{ '/tools/dashboards/eu-law-windsor-framework.html' | relative_url }}) is a small, free attempt at this, with open Commission consultations marked where the law already applies in NI.

For everyone else:

5. **Respond, briefly, with evidence.** A two-page response from a business that explains what a rule will cost it is worth more than a long essay. When response numbers are in the low hundreds, every one is read.
6. **Ask what happened.** When the outcome is late, ask your MLA to table a written question. It's a small thing that makes the loop visible.

## Where to start this winter

- **Biomethane Strategy and Policy Framework** (Department for the Economy and DAERA), open until **15 December 2026**. If you farm, process food or use gas, this one affects you.
- **The EU Emissions Trading System revision**, proposed in July 2026, with EU institutions aiming to agree it in early 2027. Northern Ireland's electricity sector sits within the EU system under the Windsor Framework.
- **The EU carbon border levy extension to downstream goods**, now being negotiated in Brussels. Not every part reaches NI, but your EU customers will feel it.
- **The May 2027 elections.** Assembly and council elections are on Thursday 6 May 2027. Ask every candidate whether they'd back a duty to close the consultation loop.

## The strongest counter-argument

*"The channels exist and they're getting better. Twelve-week consultations, published summaries, an open EU portal, a scrutiny committee that holds real inquiries, and a government that has accepted Lord Murphy's recommendations in full. If people don't respond, that's apathy, not a broken system. And Northern Ireland was given a voice, not a vote, on EU law by design."*

That's fair, and I agree on the last point: the settlement is what it is. But access is not the same as being heard. A channel without time, feedback or tracking teaches people that taking part makes no difference. Fix the loop, and the apathy will look after itself.

## What would change my mind

Published evidence that most NI consultations get a response within 12 weeks and that responses visibly change outcomes. I'd be glad to see it.
