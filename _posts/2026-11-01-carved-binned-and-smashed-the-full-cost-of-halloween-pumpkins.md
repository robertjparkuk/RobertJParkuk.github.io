Northern Ireland is the home of Halloween, and that is worth protecting. But the week after 31 October has a cost nobody counts: about 425,000 uneaten pumpkins, 900 tonnes of food, tens of tonnes of seed, easy meals for rats, sick hedgehogs and pulp on the pavements. The answer is not a no-fun council. It is to design the after-party.

## Half the pumpkins we buy are never eaten

No one counts Halloween pumpkins in Northern Ireland, so I scaled the best UK data. Hubbub's survey estimates 30.43 million UK pumpkins bought for carving, of which 15.82 million are never eaten, worth £26.7 million. Northern Ireland has 2.7% of UK households. That gives:

- about **818,000 pumpkins** bought for carving here each Halloween;
- about **425,000 never eaten**, roughly **911 tonnes** of edible flesh and 2.6 million meals;
- **£0.72m to £0.79m** of household food spending thrown away.

Around 41% of people are unsure whether supermarket "carving pumpkins" are food. They are. These figures are conservative, because Halloween is bigger here than in most of Britain.

That 911 tonnes lands on council collections in the first week of November, about 14% more household food waste than a normal fortnight. Somerset Council measured a 19% rise.

## The forgotten crop inside every lantern

A carving pumpkin holds two-thirds to one cup of seed, about 50 g once dried, and most of it is scooped straight into the bin. Northern Ireland throws away somewhere between **21 and 41 tonnes of edible seed** each Halloween. That is £166,000 to £491,000 at UK seed prices, up to 1.5 million handfuls and around 4 to 8 tonnes of protein.

The uneaten pumpkins alone hold about 210 million seeds. Many carving varieties are F1 hybrids that will not grow true, but saved seed still suits school and community growing. Oven-dried, unsalted and crushed, it makes good winter bird food.

## An easy meal for rats at the start of the cold season

Halloween falls just as rodents move towards homes for food and shelter. The British Pest Control Association and the National Pest Technicians Association both warn that doorstep pumpkins, especially once they soften and rot, attract rats and mice.

Northern Ireland's system is already stretched. BBC News NI counted more than 66,000 pest reports to councils and the Housing Executive since 2020, over 23,000 of them in Belfast. Only Belfast offers a free pest control service; most councils give advice only. Pumpkins do not cause infestations on their own, but a week of free food is the wrong signal to send.

## "Leaving it for the wildlife" does more harm than good

A growing, well-meant habit is to leave old pumpkins in woods and parks for the animals. Conservation charities are clear that this backfires:

- **Hedgehogs:** pumpkin flesh causes diarrhoea and dehydration, and rotten pumpkin can cause fatal infection, just as hedgehogs must gain weight to survive hibernation.
- **Woodland:** the Woodland Trust says dumped pumpkins damage sensitive soils, plants and fungi, attract rats, and can spread disease among deer, foxes, badgers and birds.
- **Birds:** birds can benefit if it is done properly. The RSPB's pumpkin feeder sits high, out of hedgehogs' reach, and is cleared away once it starts to rot. Never put out salted or spiced seed.

## Pulp on the pavement, pumpkins in the park

Smashed pumpkins, egging and broken bottles are part of the Halloween antisocial behaviour some Belfast neighbourhoods report every year. Pulp is a slip hazard; Staffordshire's rangers found rotting pumpkins making park paths dangerous.

Pumpkins dumped in parks, woods and verges are, in law, litter. The Litter (Northern Ireland) Order 1994, as amended in 2011, allows a fixed penalty of £75 by default. Fining well-meaning families is neither practical nor desirable, but someone still pays to clear up. Councils absorb that cost in standing budgets; Belfast's citywide street cleansing budget is about £13.8 million.

## Not a no-fun council

A council's instinct is a list of don'ts: don't carve, don't leave it out, don't feed the wildlife, or face a fine. That gets the economics backwards. Derry Halloween, Europe's largest Halloween festival and 40 this year, draws around 120,000 visitors and is reported to add about £7.4 million to the local economy. The island-wide Home of Halloween programme supported more than 830 events and 322,000 visitors in 2025.

> **Design the after-party, not the ban.** The energy that carves the pumpkin can eat, share and compost it. Leicester's annual community Pumpkin Smash, where families bring their lanterns to an allotment to smash and compost them, turns the clean-up into the fun part. Carve it, cook it, eat it, compost it.

## What would work

1. **Pumpkin Smash days** on the first weekend of November in council parks, allotments and community gardens, with compost bays and seed-roasting stalls.
2. **A one-off kerbside pumpkin collection** beside the food caddy, with candles removed, as several GB councils already offer.
3. **Seed in schools:** roast-and-taste sessions and seed-saving for spring planting.
4. **Honest labelling:** retailers stop implying carving pumpkins are not food, and add a recipe at the point of sale.
5. **One wildlife message** agreed by DAERA, the RSPB, Ulster Wildlife and councils: feeders high, seeds unsalted, nothing in the woods.
6. **Councils lead by example:** every civic pumpkin display, including Belfast City Hall's 1,000-pumpkin patch, ends in a public smash or a donation to a composting scheme.
7. **Measure it:** add a Halloween question to WRAP's Northern Ireland food-waste survey, and have councils record the post-Halloween uplift in food waste, cleansing and pest calls.

The pumpkin is a small case of a bigger public realm question: who plans for the aftermath of popular seasonal events in shared space? Councils already hold the litter, cleansing and food-waste powers. What is missing is an expectation to plan positive, low-waste use of the public realm around our festivals, not just to sweep up afterwards.
