Most boards get excellent financial information and very little else. They see revenue, margin and cash flow in detail every month. They rarely see, in any structured way, how exposed the business is to energy prices, which suppliers it can't do without, or what customers and regulators will ask of it next year.

That's the gap environmental data can fill, if it's presented properly.

## Non-financial management information

Management information doesn't have to be financial to be useful. Done properly, the data from carbon and energy work gives a board a different lens on the business:

- **Energy exposure.** How much energy you use, where, and how sensitive your costs are to price movements.
- **Supply-chain dependency.** Which materials and suppliers dominate your footprint, and often your risk.
- **Regulatory readiness.** Whether you could answer a CBAM data request, a customer questionnaire or an ESOS progress check tomorrow.
- **Commercial position.** Whether your carbon credentials help or hinder you in tenders and with key customers.

None of that appears in the management accounts. All of it can affect them.

## Why boards should care now

Accountability is moving up the organisation. ESOS progress updates now need sign-off from a board-level director. Customers increasingly want named people responsible for sustainability commitments. Lenders and insurers are asking environmental questions as part of normal credit and risk processes.

Directors don't need to become carbon experts. But they do need enough information to ask good questions and to know whether the answers hold up.

## A sceptical word on ESG

I'm openly sceptical of ESG as it's often sold: glossy reports, ratings and frameworks that generate cost without changing anything. The value isn't in the report. It's in the process of gathering the information and the decisions it leads to.

A good board pack on environmental matters is short. It shows a few key measures, how they've moved, what's driving them and what management proposes to do. It's honest about weak data. And it connects to the business plan rather than sitting in a separate sustainability silo.

## What I'd put in front of a board

For a small or mid-sized manufacturer, I'd start with one page:

1. **Total emissions and energy use**, against last year and your base year.
2. **The three biggest sources**, and what's being done about each.
3. **Upcoming obligations and customer asks**, with dates.
4. **Key risks**, such as energy price exposure, single-source suppliers or regulatory change.
5. **Decisions needed** from the board.

Reviewed quarterly, that's enough to keep environmental issues in the same conversation as everything else, which is where they belong.
