*The seas around Ireland carry transatlantic data, the island's electricity links and most of Northern Ireland's trade. The island is split between a neutral state with a small defence budget and a corner of a NATO country. That split is a sovereign choice and a political settlement. It is also a seam, and seams are where pressure goes.*

I live in Larne. About one in seven tonnes of freight through Northern Ireland's ports crosses the quay here, and Belfast handles almost two-thirds. In 2024 NI ports moved 28.4 million tonnes. Our freight, our data and the electricity we import all have to cross water.

So I want to look plainly at who watches those seas, what is changing, and what we should be asking for.

## The capability gap, in numbers

Ireland's neutrality is a long-standing policy, and NATO says it "fully respects" it. Ireland has been in NATO's Partnership for Peace since 1999. Neutrality doesn't set how much a country spends on watching its own waters, though, and here the numbers are stark.

As reported by the Irish Examiner, the European Defence Agency's latest data put Ireland's defence spending at 0.2% of GDP in 2025, about 0.25% in 2026, and 0.3% of national income on a measure that strips out multinational profits. The EDA says the EU as a whole spent 2.2% of GDP in 2025 and expects 2.4% in 2026. Ireland is lowest in the EU.

That is rising. Budget 2026 gave defence a record €1.5 billion, with €300 million of capital. Budget 2027, as reported by the Irish Times, raised it to about €1.6 billion.

The gap shows up in the kit:

- **No primary radar yet.** In September the Department of Defence signed an acquisition order with France's procurement agency for five Thales long-range radars. Deliveries begin next year, with full capability by the end of 2028. When Cabinet approved it in July, the Minister said that "for the first time" the Defence Forces would be able to monitor activity across Irish airspace.
- **Ships without crews.** As reported by the Irish Times in March, the Naval Service had eight ships but only four available for duty, because of staff shortages. Three are to get towed sonar from Thales, for about €60 million.
- **Thin air cover.** When the Russian ship *Yantar* was heading south in November 2025, the Irish Times reported that one of the Air Corps' two C295 maritime patrol aircraft was in Spain for maintenance.

Ireland's exclusive economic zone covers about 420,000 square kilometres, as reported by RTÉ. That's a lot of sea for four ships.

## What has happened in our waters

In November 2024 the *Yantar*, a Russian vessel that RTÉ reported carries mini-submarines capable of cutting or tapping cables, came just inside Ireland's zone north of Dublin. RTÉ reported it was 5 to 7 km from several subsea cables linking Ireland and Britain. An Irish naval ship sat across the nearest cables, the Air Corps watched from above, and HMS *Cattistock* shadowed it in the Irish Sea.

In April 2025 another Russian intelligence ship, the *Viktor Leonov*, passed through Ireland's zone off the west coast. Officials said, as reported by the Irish Times, that it broke no maritime laws. That is the point. Much of this activity is lawful, so the only defence is to see it, track it and make sure it knows it is being watched.

In November 2025 the UK Defence Secretary said the *Yantar* had directed lasers at RAF pilots north of Scotland. It did not enter Ireland's zone that month, but Irish ships and aircraft were put on standby in case it came south.

## What lands here

The infrastructure is getting more valuable, not less.

- **Data.** AWS's *Fastnet* cable will link Maryland and County Cork from 2028, with a design capacity of over 320 terabits a second. AWS describes it as a backup route for when other cables fail.
- **Power to the EU.** The *Celtic Interconnector*, 700 MW and 575 km from Youghal to Brittany, is due to be commissioned in the fourth quarter of 2028. In 2019 it won €530.7 million from the EU's Connecting Europe Facility. The Irish Government said at the time that once Britain left, Ireland would have no electricity link to any EU member.
- **Power to Britain.** Greenlink (500 MW, Wexford to Pembroke) began commercial operation in 2025, taking Ireland's links with Great Britain to 1 GW with the East–West Interconnector. Northern Ireland's own link is Moyle, 500 MW to Scotland.

Northern Ireland sits in the all-island Single Electricity Market, kept running by Article 9 of the Windsor Framework. A cable cut off Wexford or Youghal is a price and supply problem in Larne.

## How the money flows

It is sometimes said that EU money goes to links with France rather than Britain. The record is more nuanced, and fairer. Greenlink got EU priority status and Connecting Europe Facility money before Brexit. Since Brexit, Britain is a third country under EU energy-infrastructure rules. A link to it can only qualify as a "project of mutual interest", which needs, among other things, a "high level of convergence" between UK and EU policy and support from both governments. Even then, grants for works are a last resort for projects that the market can't fund.

That isn't a snub. It is a mechanism, and it leaves a gap: the EU has a clear route to fund Ireland–France, and a narrow one for Ireland–Britain. Northern Ireland is inside the EU electricity market but outside the EU, and its only interconnector runs to Scotland.

## What is changing

Credit where it is due. Ireland published its first National Maritime Security Strategy on 25 February 2026, after about 300 consultation responses. It proposes a national maritime security centre and an EU cable-monitoring hub for the North Atlantic, and treats cable and pipeline owners as central partners. As reported by RTÉ, it deepens ties with the UK and France; Ireland won't join the UK-led Joint Expeditionary Force but will look at its wider partner group. In July a new Act gave the Defence Forces specific functions in the territorial sea and the economic zone.

The UK's Strategic Defence Review, published on 2 June 2025, called for "securing undersea pipelines, cables, and maritime traffic". As reported by Janes, it gave the Royal Navy a new leading role in protecting undersea infrastructure. In December the MoD launched *Atlantic Bastion*, a mix of uncrewed vessels, AI, warships and aircraft, with £14 million of seed investment so far.

Northern Ireland is the only part of the island inside NATO, because the UK is a founding member. So the waters around it fall under two sets of arrangements, with different budgets, different laws and different timetables. Neither of the documents I've read says much about Northern Ireland's waters in particular.

## Practical fixes

**For the UK Government**

1. Say plainly, in public, that *Atlantic Bastion* covers the Irish Sea and the North Channel, including Moyle and the cables landing in NI.
2. Turn the new Irish partnership into a standing UK–Ireland arrangement for watching shared cables: shared tracking data and agreed hand-overs at the edge of each zone.

**For the Irish Government**

3. Keep the 2028 radar date, and fix crewing before buying more hulls. Sonar on a ship in port finds nothing.
4. Open the planned maritime security centre and EU cable hub to UK and Northern Ireland operators as partners, not observers.

**For the Executive**

5. Ask SONI, NIE Networks, the Utility Regulator and the ports to publish a short, non-sensitive scenario: what happens to NI power, data and freight if Moyle or a main Irish Sea data cable is cut for a month.
6. Make subsea risk an agenda item in Executive–Irish Government energy talks, since we share one electricity market.

**For the EU**

7. Use the North Atlantic hub and the "mutual interest" route to fund resilience on links with Britain, not just with France. Monitoring and repair capacity protects both sides of the market.

## What an NI business or council should ask

You don't need a security clearance to check your own exposure. Ask:

- **Power.** If electricity prices spiked or supply was rationed for weeks, what would we shut down first? Is our contract fixed or passed through?
- **Data.** Which data centres and routes do our cloud, payments and phone systems rely on? Do we have a second route, and have we tested it?
- **Freight.** If one port or ferry route was closed for a week, how many days of stock do we hold? Do our suppliers know our alternative route?
- **Insurance.** Does our business interruption cover include utility or telecoms failure off our premises, and is there a waiting period?
- **Plans.** For councils: is subsea and interconnector failure in our emergency plan, and have we rehearsed it with the utilities?

Ireland's neutrality is Ireland's to decide. What neither government can afford is a stretch of sea where everyone assumes someone else is watching. The fixes above are modest and most are already half-started. Finish them, and the weak flank becomes a well-watched one.
