The Chancellor's Budget of 28 October 2026 is built around energy costs. For a manufacturer in Great Britain, the headline is a 20–25% cut in electricity bills from April 2027. For a manufacturer in Northern Ireland, the headline is that the scheme stops at the Irish Sea, and the Executive has been given money to build its own. The same pattern runs through the Budget: the VAT cut on electricity, the end of Carbon Price Support and the new electricity discount are all Great Britain measures, with Northern Ireland handled by a Barnett payment and a promise. Below is what changes for NI businesses, what carries across from Great Britain, and what to do before April.

Paragraph numbers are from the Budget 2026 Red Book (HC 726). A six-page PDF of this briefing is linked at the end.

## 1. Energy: the three measures that stop at the Irish Sea

**The British Industrial Competitiveness Scheme (BICS).** From 1 April 2027, over 10,000 eligible manufacturers in Great Britain will be exempted from the indirect costs of the Renewables Obligation, Feed-in Tariffs and the Capacity Market, worth a 20–25% cut in electricity bills, with a back payment for 2026-27 (paras 4.24, 6.31). Applications are already open. Northern Ireland is not in it: the Red Book says only that "the Northern Ireland Executive is being funded to deliver a comparable scheme." There is no design, no eligibility list and no start date. NI's electricity levies are different in any case (the NI Renewables Obligation, and the Single Electricity Market's own capacity mechanism), so a copy-and-paste is not possible. This is the single biggest question for NI manufacturers to put to the Department for the Economy and to every candidate in the May 2027 Assembly election.

**VAT on domestic electricity.** The temporary 0% rate on domestic electricity, introduced on 1 October 2026, applies in Great Britain and is extended to 1 July 2027 (para 6.210). Northern Ireland's VAT on energy is constrained by the Windsor Framework, so the Executive is funded to provide "comparable support", and the Red Book expects extended VAT relief to apply in Northern Ireland from April 2027 (para 3.4). This is a household measure, but it is the clearest example in the Budget of a UK tax decision that NI cannot simply adopt.

**Carbon Price Support ends in Great Britain from April 2028** (paras 4.25, 6.208). That removes £18 per tonne of CO2 from fossil-fuelled generation in GB, worth £235 million over five years. It does nothing for Northern Ireland, because Carbon Price Support has never applied here: NI generators sit in the EU Emissions Trading System and already pay the EU carbon price. The gap between GB and NI wholesale carbon costs will narrow for GB, not for NI.

What does carry across:

- **Climate Change Levy.** The main rate on electricity and LPG is frozen from 1 April 2028 at 2027-28 levels; gas and solid fuels rise with RPI (para 6.209). The CCL is UK-wide, so this helps every NI business with an electricity bill, and makes gas relatively dearer than electricity, which is the point.
- **No new policy costs on bills** until a Cost Control Framework is set at the 2027 spending review (para 4.21). Watch how the NI Utility Regulator and the Department for the Economy treat NI's own levies in the meantime.
- **Gas Shipper Obligation scrapped** (para 6.27). The planned levy on gas bills to fund hydrogen projects will not be introduced; the Exchequer pays instead. It was a Great Britain levy, so NI loses nothing, but NI gas customers would have faced pressure to match it.
- **Workplace EV charging.** From 1 November 2026, electricity through workplace and public charge points is subject to 20% VAT and is not domestic use for the CCL (para 6.205). Fleet operators with workplace chargers should check their next bill.
- **Fuel duty.** The 5p cut and the freeze run to 1 July 2027; red diesel's 3.7p cut runs to the same date, after which it returns to its pre-2022 rate (paras 6.211, 6.212). Hauliers' £1 HGV road tax runs to 30 June 2027.

## 2. The UK carbon border charge: two changes to the detail

The UK Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism still starts on 1 January 2027 on imports of aluminium, cement, fertiliser, hydrogen, iron and steel above £50,000. The Budget changes two things.

**Default values are global for 2027 and 2028, then country-specific from 2029** (para 6.217). Importers may use either actual verified emissions from the producer or the government's published default value. For two years the default is a single global figure per good, whatever the country of origin. From 2029 it will reflect the emissions of the exporting country. For an NI importer buying from a high-carbon origin, the global default may be the cheaper option until 2029; for one buying from a cleaner supplier, verified data will usually win. Either way, the arithmetic needs doing now, and the answer may change in 2029.

**Refined products are proposed for the scope** (paras 4.86, 6.218). The government "intends to expand the scope of the CBAM to include refined products, subject to consultation." The policy is aimed at refineries in Pembrokeshire, Cheshire, Hampshire and the Humber. Northern Ireland has no refinery and imports all its road fuel and heating oil, so an NI fuel importer or distributor could become a CBAM registrant without any change to its business. The consultation is the moment to make that point.

The Budget says nothing about the EU CBAM, the Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive or linking the UK and EU emissions trading schemes. For an NI exporter the position is unchanged: your EU customer is the EU declarant, and they will keep asking for your embedded-emissions data.

## 3. Waste: a delay for incinerators and a rise in Landfill Tax

**Energy from waste stays out of the UK ETS for now.** The Budget scores the "delay of the inclusion of energy from waste in the ETS" at £125 million in 2028-29 rising to £490 million the year after (Table 6.1, item 96). DESNZ had already dropped the 2028 start date in August; the Red Book gives no new one. For councils, this removes a carbon charge on residual waste that was due to feed through to gate fees from 2028. For anyone modelling an arc21-style residual-waste contract, the base case has changed.

**Landfill Tax** rises by RPI in England and Northern Ireland, with the lower rate rising by the same cash amount so the two rates do not drift apart (para 6.221). Landfill operators can claim more of their liability against the Landfill Communities Fund, up from 5.3% to 8.3% (para 6.222).

**Plastic Packaging Tax.** From 1 April 2030, mechanically recycled plastic will need mandatory certification to count towards the 30% recycled-content threshold (para 6.220). Any NI packer or converter relying on supplier declarations has three years to move to certified material.

## 4. Investment, trade and red tape

- **Capital allowances** (paras 2.54, 6.251). The main-rate first-year allowance rises from 40% to 50% for spending from 1 January 2027; the writing-down allowance falls by two points to 12% from April 2027. Full expensing stays. For most companies the business case for energy-efficiency plant is unchanged; for sole traders and partnerships, who cannot use full expensing, the 12% rate makes long-life kit slower to write off.
- **Tariff suspensions** (paras 6.30, 6.32). Fertiliser tariffs are suspended until 31 December 2027 and kerosene until 31 December 2028, both explicitly aimed at rural and Northern Ireland households and farms. Existing suspensions across chemicals and plastics are extended, and a new application window runs from 11 November 2026 to 3 February 2027. A fertiliser importer will pay no tariff and a carbon charge on the same bag.
- **Company reporting.** The government expects up to £290 million a year of savings from "a more proportionate company reporting framework" (para 4.52), and a Better Regulation Executive is being set up in the Treasury (para 4.53). UK statutory reporting is getting lighter at the same time as customers, lenders and EU buyers are asking for more environmental data. The report is not the point; the data behind it is.
- **Critical Chemicals Resilience Fund** (para 6.92). The £350 million fund for strategically important chemicals sites launches by the end of 2026.
- **Northern Ireland specifically** (paras 4.158–4.160). £270 million extra for the Executive through Barnett; £7.5 million for the Cyber AI Hub at Queen's; £1 million for a Northern Ireland Investment Summit, pitched on NI's "dual access to the UK internal market and EU market". The Red Book is enthusiastic about dual access. Sections 1 and 2 above show what it costs.

## 5. Five things to do before April

1. **Ask the Department for the Economy what the NI electricity scheme will look like**, and when. If you are an energy-intensive manufacturer, say so in writing now, through your trade body as well as directly.
2. **Run the CBAM default-value calculation** for every CBAM good you import from outside the UK, under both the 2027-28 global default and your suppliers' actual figures. Keep both.
3. **Respond to the refined-products consultation** if you import or distribute fuel. Northern Ireland's position is unlike anywhere else in the UK.
4. **Check the capital-allowance timing** on any plant you planned to buy: ordering after 1 January 2027 attracts the 50% first-year allowance.
5. **Re-run residual-waste cost models** without a 2028 UK ETS charge, and watch for a new date.

## For the self-employed and small practices

Three measures affect sole traders directly. Tax-free mileage rises permanently to 55p for the first 10,000 miles and 35p above (para 6.213). Anyone joining Making Tax Digital for income tax in April 2027 gets no late-filing penalty points on their first four quarterly updates (para 6.152). Business Asset Disposal Relief falls to a £500,000 lifetime limit from 6 April 2027 (para 6.261), which will bring forward some owner-managed business sales in Northern Ireland before the deadline.
