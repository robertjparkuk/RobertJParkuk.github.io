The public realm is everything we share and don't own: streets and pavements, verges and hedgerows, parks, beaches, rivers, car parks, the bins at the end of the lane. We notice it mainly when it goes wrong. Fly-tipping in a lay-by. A river that turns green in July. A town centre that feels tired.

Most of the time we treat these as separate problems, owned by separate departments with separate budgets. I think that's the root of the trouble.

## Four things that hold a place together

When I look at a place that works, I see four things pulling in the same direction.

- **Land.** Soil, water and green space that are cared for rather than just cut back. A verge mown once a year instead of six times. Drains that go where they should.
- **Waste.** Collection that is reliable, litter that is picked up quickly, and enforcement that makes dumping a bad bet.
- **Duty.** Somebody clearly responsible, with the powers and the money to act, and an obligation to keep improving rather than simply not getting worse.
- **Civic pride.** People who feel the place is theirs, and who report, volunteer and complain because they care.

Take any one away and the others start to slip. Pride fades when nobody seems responsible. Duties go unmet when nobody is watching. Land and waste problems compound each other: litter blocks drains, and neglected corners attract dumping.

## The law is part of the problem

Responsibility for the public realm is spread across more than thirty pieces of legislation, several of them Victorian. Litter sits in one Act, highways in another, and parks, public toilets, street trading and dog control in others again. Each has its own duty-holder, its own standard and its own enforcement route. Spending on shared spaces is usually discretionary, so it is the first thing cut when budgets tighten. No area is entitled to any minimum standard, and there's no consistent way of telling whether places are getting better or worse.

I've been drafting a private Public Realm Bill to tackle this. The first version, circulated in August, was too big. After a hard scrutiny exercise I've reworked it into a much shorter framework Bill, with the older amenity laws left to a separate consolidation through the Law Commission. The ideas at its heart haven't changed:

- **A clear definition.** The public realm covers the streets, parks, squares, seafronts, libraries, public toilets, memorials and other places people use in common, whoever owns them.
- **Stewardship and continuous improvement.** Public bodies that own, manage or affect those places should have to keep improving them at the lowest whole-life cost, cooperate where their responsibilities overlap, and be candid about condition, safety and funding.
- **A minimum public realm entitlement.** Every area should be able to expect the basics: safe, lit walking and wheeling routes, green space, public toilets and drinking water, cleanliness, seating and shade, and access to a library and somewhere to be active.
- **More for the places furthest behind.** An enhanced entitlement for left-behind, coastal, rural and island communities, with public realm funding per head at least 25% higher there than elsewhere.
- **Counting what matters.** Spending decisions should be appraised in a way that gives weight to benefits that can't easily be priced, in line with the Treasury's 2026 Green Book, which no longer treats the highest benefit-cost ratio as the only test.

Most of this is devolved, so the framework is offered to each devolved government to adopt and adapt rather than imposed. Northern Ireland is different again: no mayors, a separate rating system, councils with a general power of competence, and the Department for Communities and Department for Infrastructure leading on regeneration and roads. Any version for here has to be built around that.

You don't need new legislation to start acting on these ideas, though. Councils can adopt them as local standards tomorrow.

## What good guardianship looks like

Guardianship is a better word than maintenance. Maintenance is about keeping things as they are. Guardianship is about handing a place on in better shape than you found it. In practice, that tends to mean:

- **Joining up the budgets.** The cost of a blocked gully, a flooded road and an insurance claim all land in different places. Look at them together and the cheap fix upstream becomes obvious.
- **Managing land for nature as well as tidiness.** Fewer cuts, cutting hedges in the right season and at the right height, leaving buffer strips by rivers. These often cost less, not more. My [Land Repair Helix]({{ '/tools/land-repair-helix.html' | relative_url }}) pairs common problems with low-cost fixes.
- **Making enforcement visible.** Fly-tipping falls when people see that it is investigated and punished. In Northern Ireland, pollution and dumping can be reported to the NIEA on 0800 80 70 60, and councils handle litter and fly-tipping on public land.
- **Measuring what residents notice.** Response times, repeat problems, and how people rate their area, not just tonnes collected or miles swept.
- **Giving people a way in.** Friends-of groups, adopt-a-spot schemes and community clean-ups work best when the council backs them with equipment, insurance and a named contact.

## Businesses have a part too

The public realm isn't only a council matter. Businesses own land that faces onto it, generate waste that passes through it, and depend on it for their customers and staff. A factory with a scruffy boundary, a car park that floods the road, or packaging that ends up in the hedge is part of the problem. A business that looks after its frontage, its drainage and its waste is part of the answer, and it shows in staff pride and in how the community sees the firm.

## Where to start

If you're a council officer or an elected member, pick one street, one park or one stretch of river and map who is responsible for every part of it. You'll quickly find the gaps. If you're a business, walk your own boundary with fresh eyes and ask what a neighbour would see.
