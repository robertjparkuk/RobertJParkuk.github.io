## The argument in brief

On 14 July 2026 Lincolnshire County Council's Executive voted to bring back booking at all 11 of its household waste recycling centres. Reform UK has run the council since May 2025. The next day South Holland District Council voted to object, with one councillor calling the plan unfair to people who work. The county's own report puts the saving at about £1.02 million a year, and that figure assumes 20% less waste arrives at the sites.

That assumption is the weak point. Booking systems, van bans, permit caps and residency checks save money by turning waste away. Councils say the waste turned away belongs to traders and people from outside the area. Few councils publish evidence for that, and no government measures where turned-away waste ends up.

Meanwhile England recorded 1.26 million fly-tipping incidents in 2024/25, up 9% on the year, and 37% were on pavements and roads. The tip is tidier. The verge, the lay-by and the river bank pay for it. Councils have real reasons to restrict access, and this piece sets them out fairly. Still, the case for restriction rests on savings that are counted and costs that are not.

*This piece sets out the arguments on each side; the recommendations are the author's. Facts checked to 30 October 2026.*

## The lifecycle at a glance

Waste leaks out at two points before it reaches a recycling centre: a cheap unlicensed carrier, or a gate that turns it away. The council's waste budget counts the tonnes refused; the clean-up lands on other budgets.

- No UK government measures where turned-away waste goes, and Westminster has said in writing that it will not.

- Reform councils say booking cuts waste and favours residents. Lib Dem councils protect opening hours. Both are answering the same cost pressure.

- England's DIY rule is the only national limit on what councils can do at the gate. Scotland, Wales and NI have none.

- In NI, residency rules bite hardest along the border and at council boundaries. No party has an Assembly-level policy.

## Where the pillars compete

- **A tidy site against a dirty verge.** Lincolnshire's £1.02m saving depends on 20% less tonnage. Some will be trade waste, rightly refused. Some will be household waste that ends up elsewhere. The saving shows in the waste budget; the clean-up lands on street cleansing, the NIEA, landowners and rivers. Landfill tax, which applies in NI, rises by RPI from 1 Apr 2027 (Budget, 28 Oct 2026). Each refused tonne will save councils a little more.

- **Recycling rates against total waste.** Turning waste away can lift a site's recycling percentage while dumping rises outside it. The percentage looks better. The environment may not be.

- **Queues and safety against access.** Lincolnshire reports queues backing onto the A17, and police once asked for a site to close. Booking fixes this by rationing. An extra day's opening would cost about £1.5m a year. The council has promised a phone booking line.

- **Van-only households.** Mid and East Antrim allows a household whose only vehicle is a van 12 visits a year; Ards and North Down allows six. A tradesperson's family clearing a house is rationed like a business.

- **Digital exclusion.** Research for Defra found phone bookings were only 2–4% of the total. Councils read that as low demand. It may show people giving up.

## What the evidence shows on booking and fly-tipping

The honest answer is that nobody knows. The 2022 study for Defra found no published evidence either way and warned that Covid made the effect impossible to isolate. Its survey of 41 councils recorded their own views: councils with booking did not blame it for fly-tipping, and councils without it were more sceptical. West Northamptonshire reported over 21,000 visits in the first weeks after its 3 November 2025 launch and credited booking with shorter queues; it published no fly-tipping comparison. Shropshire scrapped booking for cars in February 2025. Councils sometimes treat the absence of evidence as proof the system is safe. It shows only that nobody has measured.

## What it means for Northern Ireland firms and councils

- **The rules differ across all 11 councils.** Ards and North Down require booking for everyone. Newry, Mourne and Down require it only for vehicles over 1.95 metres. The table below sets out each council's rules as published.

- **Trade outlets vary by council.** Derry and Strabane, Mid Ulster, and Armagh, Banbridge and Craigavon take paid trade waste. Mid and East Antrim's commercial permits cover only cardboard, garden waste and "old for new" items. Construction and demolition waste is excluded.

- **NI cannot measure the problem.** Belfast's own October 2025 report says councils record fly-tipping differently. The NIEA's 17.72 tonnes for 2025 covers only the large incidents it handles. The UK Budget's funding for clearing six illegal waste sites was not stated to cover NI (28 Oct 2026).

- **The border adds cost.** In 2020 about one vehicle in ten at Derry's Pennyburn site was Republic-registered. Monaghan's civic amenity sites charge €4 just to enter.

- **Duty of care falls on householders.** A £300 penalty awaits anyone whose waste is dumped by an unlicensed carrier. Tighter access pushes households towards cheap "man with a van" offers.

## Practical examples

- **Ards and North Down: booking for everyone.** Since 4 September 2023 all nine sites need an online booking. Vans and double-axle trailers are capped at six visits per address a year, and visitors must show proof of residency. The council page offers no phone route (read 28 Oct 2026). [Conditions of use](https://www.ardsandnorthdown.gov.uk/HRC-Conditions-of-use)

- **Mid and East Antrim: permits at Larne.** Van-only households need a permit and a booked slot at Larne, Ballymena or Carrickfergus, up to 12 visits a year. A missed booking counts against the cap unless cancelled an hour ahead (read 28 Oct 2026). [Council page](https://www.midandeastantrim.gov.uk/resident/waste-recycling/hrc-online-booking-system)

- **Derry and Strabane: a paid door for traders.** Commercial operators can use council sites with a voucher bought in advance, from £51.50 for a small van to £113.30 for a 7.5-tonne lorry, excluding VAT. That gives small traders a legal outlet (read 28 Oct 2026). [Commercial charges](https://recycling.derrystrabane.com/?p=6580)

- **Newry, Mourne and Down: book only the big vehicles.** Cars, small vans and single-axle trailers can still turn up. Vehicles over 1.95 metres book a free slot online or by phone, a scheme launched in January 2023. [Council launch, 6 Jan 2023](https://www.newrymournedown.org/council-launches-new-online-booking-system-for-large-vehicle-access-to-household-recycling-centres)

- **Omagh plans booking with safeguards.** Fermanagh and Omagh's director of environment said planned booking for Omagh-area sites would allow unlimited visits and a phone option. It forms part of a £4 million safety programme (reported 11 Nov 2025). [We Are Tyrone](https://wearetyrone.com/?p=381623)

- **The bill for the big dumps.** English councils spent £19.3 million clearing 52,000 fly-tips of tipper-lorry size or larger in 2024/25. Small-van loads were the most common size, at 31% (25 Feb 2026). [Defra statistics](https://www.gov.uk/government/statistics/fly-tipping-statistics-for-england/fly-tipping-statistics-for-england-2024-to-2025)

## Recommendations

1.  **Councils: make booking optional for cars.** Keep slots for busy times and allow drive-up otherwise. Shropshire shows it can work.

2.  **Councils: replace van bans and low caps with free, simple permits.** Allow at least 12 visits a year, with more on request, and take applications by phone and in libraries.

3.  **DAERA: extend England's free DIY allowance to NI by regulation.** Two bags or one fitted item per visit is cheaper than clearing it from a lane.

4.  **Councils: publish fly-tipping figures for 12 months before and after any access change, by ward and on a common method.** A saving claimed without this has not been shown.

5.  **DAERA and councils: agree an NI-wide access standard.** Set minimum hours, a non-digital booking route, reciprocal access at boundaries and van rights for households.

6.  **DAERA and Defra: tie part of EPR payments to measured access.** Councils that cut access while fly-tipping rises should have to explain why.

7.  **NI Executive and Dublin: open talks on cross-border civic amenity pricing.** Price gaps drive waste across the border.

8.  **Legislators: write a public realm duty into any future bill.** Require councils to assess effects on streets, verges and waterways before cutting waste services.
