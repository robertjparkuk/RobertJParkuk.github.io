---
title: "The report is the by-product: what a carbon inventory is really for"
tags: [Carbon, ESG]
---

Ask most businesses why they're measuring their carbon footprint and you'll hear one of three answers: a customer asked for it, a tender required it, or a regulation is coming. All fair reasons. But if the only thing you get out of the exercise is a number in a report, you've paid for the least valuable part.

## The process does the work

To build a carbon inventory you have to ask questions that rarely get asked. Where does our energy actually go? What do we know about the materials we buy, and the people who make them? Which of our customers are going to need this data from us — and when?

Those questions cut across finance, operations, procurement and sales. Answering them means people who don't normally talk to each other start comparing notes. Suppliers get a different kind of conversation from the usual one about price and delivery. That is where the commercial value tends to show up.

## Information the board doesn't have yet

Most boards are well served with financial information and poorly served with everything else. A carbon inventory, done properly, produces non-financial management information: exposure to energy prices, dependence on particular suppliers, readiness for customer and regulatory demands.

None of that appears in the management accounts. All of it matters.

## Why this matters more in Northern Ireland

Manufacturers here often sell into both the UK and EU markets, which means keeping an eye on two sets of carbon rules rather than one. That makes good, well-understood data more valuable — and a box-ticking approach more risky.

## Where to start

You don't need to begin with a full-scale reporting framework. Start by understanding where your emissions are likely to sit, which customers or rules will ask about them first, and what data you already hold. The report will follow. The understanding is the point.
