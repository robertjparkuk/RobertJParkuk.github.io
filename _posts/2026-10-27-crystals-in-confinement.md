In 2002 I grew a crystal in the shape of a sea urchin. The question behind it was simple: how do pores control what minerals do? More than twenty years later, that question sits behind some of the hardest problems on my desk, from cleaning up contaminated land to capturing forever chemicals. And it leads to an uncomfortable fact about Northern Ireland: we are the only part of the UK without a contaminated land regime in force.

## Where it began

My doctoral research in materials chemistry at Queen Mary University of London studied biominerals. From 2000 to 2003 I worked with Eva Loste in Professor Fiona Meldrum's group. We cast sea urchin skeleton plates in polymer, dissolved away the original mineral, and grew single crystals inside the sponge-like moulds that were left. The crystals took on the urchin's shape: the mould alone set the form.

Three papers came from that work:

- **Synthesis of single crystals of calcite with complex morphologies.** Park and Meldrum, *Advanced Materials*, 2002. The urchin casts shaped crystals of calcium carbonate, and also of strontium and lead sulfates, lead carbonate, salt and copper sulfate.
- **Shape-constraint as a route to calcite single crystals with complex morphologies.** Park and Meldrum, *Journal of Materials Chemistry*, 2004. Under double diffusion, the membrane alone decided the crystal's shape.
- **Precipitation of calcium carbonate in confinement.** Loste, Park, Warren and Meldrum, *Advanced Functional Materials*, 2004. Calcium carbonate grown in very small confined volumes.

The work is still cited. Professor Meldrum's 2020 review of crystallisation in confinement uses the urchin result as its lead example of crystals shaped purely by the space around them.

## Three principles that travel

- **Pore size decides what forms.** In very narrow pores, a different crystal form of the same mineral can win.
- **Transport decides where and when it forms.** How chemicals diffuse through a porous material controls where crystals start and what they turn into.
- **Size matching decides what is captured.** The same logic governs which molecules a porous material will hold on to.

## Where those principles turn up now

**Contaminated land.** Several clean-up methods are really confinement problems. Microbes or enzymes can be used to grow calcium carbonate that locks lead, cadmium or arsenic into soil, but whether those metals stay put for decades depends on which crystal form grows and the chemistry of the pores. Permeable reactive barriers fail when their pores clog. Sorbents mixed into soil work, or don't, depending on pore size and surface charge.

**Forever chemicals (PFAS).** There are more than 9,000 PFAS compounds. Adsorption is the most cost-effective way to remove them, and engineered porous materials outperform activated carbon on capacity and speed. Pore size, surface charge and competition from carbonate in the water are the variables that matter.

**F-gases.** Some refrigerant gases trap thousands of times more heat than carbon dioxide. Porous materials can separate refrigerant blends so the parts can be reused rather than released, by matching pore to molecule.

**Carbon removal.** At Carbfix in Iceland, most of the carbon dioxide injected into basalt turns into calcite within two years. Pore architecture is one of the factors that controls how fast.

**Metal–organic frameworks.** The 2025 Nobel Prize in Chemistry went to Susumu Kitagawa, Richard Robson and Omar Yaghi "for the development of metal–organic frameworks": crystals built like scaffolding, with rooms of a designed size and chemistry. They ran in parallel with our work and rest on the same idea, controlling chemistry by controlling space. BASF now makes them at commercial scale for carbon capture.

## The Northern Ireland problem

England, Scotland and Wales have run a statutory contaminated land regime since 2000 and 2001. Northern Ireland's equivalent, Part III of the Waste and Contaminated Land (Northern Ireland) Order 1997, was enacted but has never been brought into force, and DAERA says no timetable has been agreed.

After Ministerial agreement, DAERA tried to commission a study of the costs and resources needed to start the regime. It received no tenders. Officials are considering next steps, and DAERA says the project may be completed in 2027.

In the meantime, contamination is dealt with mostly through planning, when land is sold or redeveloped. Sites that aren't coming forward can sit unexamined. Buyers, lenders and anyone acquiring an owner-managed business with land carry more of the risk, because the backstop that exists elsewhere in the UK doesn't exist here. I've set out what that means for businesses in [Contaminated land in Northern Ireland: the regime that never started]({{ '/blog/2026/10/contaminated-land-in-northern-ireland/' | relative_url }}).

PFAS makes the gap more pressing. Defra's first PFAS Plan, published in February 2026, reports PFAS in about 80% of surface water and about half of groundwater samples tested in England, and commits to guidance on legacy PFAS on contaminated land. That plan is mainly for England. Northern Ireland has not yet set its own approach.

## What I'm asking for

Complete the cost and resource work, then set a date to commence Part III, with a phased start, funding for councils, and PFAS guidance built in from day one. A working regime gives landowners, lenders and investors certainty as well as protecting health. With Assembly elections due in May 2027, now is the time to make the case. I'm preparing a briefing for MLAs and councillors and would welcome views from businesses and councils.

## How this shapes my work

I don't run a laboratory, and I'm clear about that. What the science gives me is the ability to read the primary research and explain it plainly to the people who have to act on it:

- **Land and transactions.** Informed scrutiny of remediation proposals and land liability in due diligence, with specialist firms doing the site work.
- **Manufacturers.** CBAM and CSRD advice, including process emissions, F-gas exposure and the credibility of carbon removal claims.
- **Policy.** Public affairs on contaminated land, PFAS and F-gases, grounded in the chemistry.

Some of the links in this piece are direct and some are open questions. Laboratory results aren't field-proven technologies, and none of this claims that our papers caused later developments. But the question we asked in 2002, how pores control what minerals do, turns out to be one worth asking about land Northern Ireland has never had to clean up.
