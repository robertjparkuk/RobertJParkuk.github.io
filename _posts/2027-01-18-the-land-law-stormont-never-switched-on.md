## The argument in brief

On 22 May 2026, DAERA Minister Andrew Muir (Alliance) briefed Foyle MLAs on Mobuoy, which he called one of the largest illegal dumping sites in western Europe. He promised a final remediation strategy, a new cost estimate and a business case. Four days later the SDLP complained that the site still had "not one penny in place". That row shows the wider gap. Northern Ireland has no working legal regime for historically contaminated land.

Part III of the Waste and Contaminated Land (Northern Ireland) Order 1997 would create that regime. It is the NI version of Part 2A of the Environmental Protection Act 1990, which has applied in Great Britain since 2000–2001. Almost 30 years on, Part III has never been commenced. DAERA's project page, last updated on 14 April 2026, says a tender for a report on the cost and resources of the regime received no bids, officials are considering next steps, and the work may be completed in 2027. In practice, contamination is dealt with when someone applies for planning permission. Land that nobody develops is not dealt with at all.

This piece argues that the Executive should agree to commence Part III before the May 2027 Assembly election, phased in and with capped funding. The case against is serious. Councils face costs, homeowners could face blight and liability, and remediation money competes with money for housing. Those risks can be managed. Doing nothing is also a choice, and the public pays for it later.

*This piece sets out the arguments on each side; the recommendations are the author's. Facts checked to 30 October 2026.*

- **The real argument is about money.** Muir and the SDLP both look to London for help. No party has costed an NI-funded regime. The £1.5bn offered to the Executive on 28 Oct 2026 creates room to argue for a budget line.

- **Mobuoy is treated as a one-off crime scene.** NIAO puts the wider legacy at 466 sites, with an estimated £250m clean-up cost (2015 estimate).

- **"Polluter pays" has no live tool.** Party calls for it sit uneasily with having no statutory power to make polluters pay for historic land.

- **Councils are absent from the debate.** In 2018 they preferred commencement with a capped capital fund. SOLACE NI backed none of the options (DAERA, 2019).

## Where the pillars compete

- **Clean-up versus homes.** NIAO's best Mobuoy estimate is £107m, inside a £17m–£700m range (Jul 2025). Every extra pound spent digging out waste cannot go on social homes under the 100,000-home ambition.

- **Polluter pays versus innocent owners.** Under GB Part 2A, current owners or occupiers carry liability where no polluter can be found (s78F). DAERA's 2019 report warned NI homeowners could be exposed the same way.

- **Removal versus carbon (E versus E).** Dublin Airport says even low PFAS levels force soil to be shipped abroad; MetroLink may need over 300,000 m³ exported (May 2026). Digging and hauling moves the risk and adds emissions.

- **Certainty versus blight.** A public register can stigmatise land. Without one, land stays idle because its condition is unknown (DAERA, 2019). Both outcomes hurt regeneration and old industrial towns.

- **Enforcement versus deterrence.** Waste crime fines from 2019 to 2024 came to under £1m, while legal disposal of the same waste would have cost £17.2m (NIAO). Crime pays, and the taxpayer covers the difference.

## What it means for Northern Ireland firms and councils

- **Hidden risk on old land.** About 12,000 sites appear in DAERA's unvalidated Historic Land Use Database.

- **Expansion triggers the planning route.** Expect requests for a Preliminary Risk Assessment, then possibly quantitative work, a remediation strategy and verification.

- **Few remedies against neighbours.** With no statutory regime, a firm hit by contamination from next door has little leverage.

- **PFAS is next.** Airfields, fire training grounds, metal finishing and textiles are the likely sources; check against the CL:AIRE sector list.

- **Councils would be the regulators.** Environmental health teams need staff, budget and early notice.

- **Cross-border comparison.** Ireland must register contaminated sites under EU law, and investors will compare the two systems.

## Practical examples

- **A £4,000 fine and a site still dirty.** NIEA found about 1,000 tonnes of illegal waste at a County Down site between 2017 and 2019. The operator pleaded guilty in September 2019 and was fined £4,000; legal disposal would have cost £177,780. The site has not been remediated ([NIAO, 3 Jul 2025](https://www.niauditoffice.gov.uk/files/niauditoffice/documents/2025-07/NI%20Audit%20Office%20Report%20-%20Waste%20Crime%20in%20NI.pdf)).

- **A polluter with nothing to pay.** At a County Tyrone site holding over 5,300 tonnes, the bankrupt offender got community service and a £1 confiscation order in 2018 ([NIAO, 3 Jul 2025](https://www.niauditoffice.gov.uk/files/niauditoffice/documents/2025-07/NI%20Audit%20Office%20Report%20-%20Waste%20Crime%20in%20NI.pdf)). Without Part III, nobody else can be made to clean up.

- **Cross-border waste still in the ground.** About 270,000 tonnes from the Republic of Ireland were dumped at 17 NI sites by 2004. Repatriation began in 2010, yet the five largest sites still hold about 137,000 tonnes. NIEA has spent £492,000 on 12 sites ([NIAO, 3 Jul 2025](https://www.niauditoffice.gov.uk/files/niauditoffice/documents/2025-07/NI%20Audit%20Office%20Report%20-%20Waste%20Crime%20in%20NI.pdf)).

- **Mobuoy, uncovered in 2012.** The site holds an estimated 1.6 million tonnes of waste, 635,000 tonnes of it illegally deposited (NIAO, Jul 2025). DAERA is moving towards a final strategy, then a new cost estimate and business case, with no dates given ([DAERA, 22 May 2026](https://www.daera-ni.gov.uk/news/daera-minister-updates-political-leaders-mobuoy-remediation)).

- **PFAS soil at Dublin Airport.** daa found PFAS at a former fire training ground, first seen in 2023 during apron works. On one underpass job it was cheaper to class 150 tonnes as contaminated than to test it ([Irish Examiner, 27 May 2026](https://www.irishexaminer.com/news/arid-41852241.html)).

- **England maps PFAS source sectors.** CL:AIRE published 13 site profiles for the Environment Agency's PFAS Risk Screening Project, covering metal finishing, paper and textiles among others ([LexisNexis, 29 Jul 2026](https://www.lexisnexis.co.uk/legal/news/cl-aire-publishes-pfas-site-profiles-for-potential-pfas-source-sectors)). NI manufacturers in those sectors can use them to screen their own sites.

## Recommendations

1.  **Executive: agree before May 2027 to commence Part III in phases.** It gives a legal footing that does not depend on someone choosing to develop a site.

2.  **DAERA: produce the cost and resources assessment in-house or re-tender now.** Waiting until 2027 pushes the decision past the election.

3.  **Department of Finance and Executive: create a capped, risk-ranked capital fund (the 2019 "Option 4").** Councils asked for this; England shows duties without money stall. The £1.5bn three-year offer of 28 Oct 2026 is one possible source.

4.  **DAERA: issue NI statutory guidance that protects innocent homeowners.** Exclusion and hardship tests keep the regime aimed at risk, not blight.

5.  **DAERA and NIEA: validate the Historic Land Use Database and add PFAS screening.** Good data lowers costs for councils and lenders.

6.  **DAERA: publish its response and timeline to NIAO's July 2025 recommendation.** The audit already asked for it.

7.  **Councils: draft inspection strategies now and link them to local development plans.** Twelve months after commencement is a short time.

8.  **Firms: commission a desk study of your own site and review leases and insurance.** Knowing your liabilities first is cheaper than finding out when a buyer asks.
