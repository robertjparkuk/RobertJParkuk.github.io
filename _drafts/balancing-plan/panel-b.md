## Part A. CBAM importer logic, items 1 to 5 (B3 and B5 joint check)

**Overall:** the logic is mostly right, and better than most of the consultancy material around. It has four problems a customs manager would spot straight away. Item 1 also contradicts the live CBAM Desk page on the most sensitive point.

### A1. EU goods moved into NI: item 1 states as settled law what the CBAM Desk page calls "unconfirmed"

- Item 1, case 2: *"Goods arriving in Northern Ireland from Ireland or the rest of the EU count as UK CBAM imports under the Finance Act 2026."*
- CBAM Desk (live): Finance Act 2026, s.144(5)(a) treats "a Union good that is imported into the United Kingdom as a result of its entry into Northern Ireland" as an import; the importer is "the person on whose behalf the good is imported" (s.146(4)). *"This is my reading of the Act. HMRC's guidance does not yet cover this case … the position is **unconfirmed**. I asked HMRC's CBAM policy team on 16 November 2026."*
- GOV.UK's threshold-exclusions page (updated 10 Nov 2026, check 2) does not deal with goods moved from Ireland or the EU into NI. It does exclude "Union goods exported from Northern Ireland into the EU and reimported" within three years in the same state, which supports the view that Union goods entering NI can be in scope. It does not settle the point.
- **B3:** "Put the same caveat in item 1 as on the Desk page, word for word. An NI buyer reading item 1 will think HMRC has confirmed it. If HMRC replies the other way, it is the article people will screenshot."
- **B5:** "Politically this is the 'carbon border on the island of Ireland' story. Write it flat and sourced: what the Act says, what HMRC has not yet said, what the government intends. No adjectives."

### A2. The EU-origin exemption: announced, not law, and wider than "EU"

- Item 1 says the government "intends to exempt EU-origin goods from 1 January 2027". The live Desk page says the intention covers goods originating in **the EU, the EEA and Switzerland**, and that the EU's exemption for UK goods is expected from 1 January 2028. Item 1 should use the same wording. **B4:** "The EEA part matters to me: Norwegian aluminium through a Dublin or Rotterdam stockholder is a common route."
- None of this is law until regulations are made. The panel could not find the GOV.UK text of the 20 November statement (check 9). Trade press on 9 November reported speculation about backdating, and that the draft statement said exemptions would follow "subject to respective laws and procedures" (check 7). Link the official source, and give the date the regulations are expected, if one has been given.
- **B5:** the 1 Jan 2027 (UK) and 1 Jan 2028 (EU) dates create a one-year asymmetry. Say so: it is the single most useful fact for an NI firm buying from the EU and selling into it.

### A3. The £50,000 test: two tests, and "UK-origin" goods are out

- Item 1 says "if your CBAM goods from outside the UK pass **£50,000 in any 12 months** … you must register". Item 2 says the same. GOV.UK (check 1) sets **two** tests from 1 January 2027:
  - a **forward-looking** test: you expect to import CBAM goods "worth £50,000 or more within the next 30 days"; and
  - a **backward-looking** test, checked "on the first day of each month", on the previous 12 months.
  - Where both apply, the earliest date counts. Goods imported before 1 January 2027 do not count.
  The live Desk page already describes both tests correctly. Items 1 and 2 should match it.
- GOV.UK (check 2): goods of **UK origin** are exempt and do not count towards the threshold, and the test is the UK's **non-preferential rules of origin**. So **GB-rolled plate bought through a Dublin stockholder is out**, whatever the law turns out to be on Union goods entering NI. Item 1 should add this as a fourth case. It is common in NI fabrication supply chains, and it is a correct, reassuring answer.
- **Value:** neither GOV.UK page says how "value" is measured for the threshold (checks 1 and 2). For an EU-to-NI movement there is no customs declaration, so there is no customs value. Item 1's worked example adds invoice prices that include the Dublin stockholder's margin and delivery. Add: *"HMRC has not said how value is measured for goods with no customs declaration; I have used invoice value as a prudent proxy."*
- Other exclusions worth one line in item 2: returned goods relief, temporary admission, and inward and outward processing (check 2).

### A4. Origin versus consignment

- Item 1 gets the core point right: "steel that originated outside the EU (for example Turkish or Indian plate held in a Dublin yard) stays in scope either way." **B3:** "Good. Add one sentence on why: cutting to length, shot-blasting or priming in a Dublin yard does not usually change non-preferential origin. The mill certificate decides, not the delivery note."
- **B5 (missing, important):** Turkish or Indian plate that entered the EU through Ireland has, since 1 January 2026, carried **EU CBAM**, payable by the EU importer, the Irish stockholder. When it then moves into NI it may also bear **UK CBAM**. Item 1 should say plainly that this is a possible double charge. It should also say that UK carbon price relief is aimed at carbon prices paid in the country of origin, so it is not clear that EU CBAM certificates bought by the Irish importer would count. *Flag as unverified; ask HMRC in the same query as A1.* This is exactly what an NI buyer needs to raise with a Dublin supplier now, and it is a strong reason to call Robert.
- Turkey is in a customs union with the EU, but steel (former ECSC products) is outside it. That does not make Turkish steel EU-origin for either CBAM. One line in item 1 heads off a common misunderstanding.

### A5. Other points in items 1 to 5

- **Item 1, "about £19 a tonne of steel from the US, Brazil, China or India … with little or no carbon price relief":** defaults are global for 2027 and 2028 (items 2 and 3), so the country does not change the default. The countries are named only because of relief. **B5:** China's national ETS was extended to steel, cement and aluminium in 2025, so "little or no relief" for China needs care. Say "little relief is likely, because prices are low and allocation largely free *[verify]*", or drop China from the list.
- **Item 2:** the outline leaves the registration deadline and first return as "[verify]", but the live Desk page already says "Register by 31 January 2028; the first return and payment for 2027 are due by 31 May 2028." Use the Desk's figures and re-verify them against HMRC on the day. A draft that is less sure than the live site looks careless. Also check whether UK CBAM counts indirect emissions for each sector. The outline's "where required" is right to hedge, but name the sectors.
- **Item 3:** the decision rule is good and honest ("for most small NI importers in 2027, it does not pay"). Add that steel defaults may carry a UK mark-up. The Desk page notes that "fertiliser defaults carry no UK mark-up", which implies others do. The mark-up raises the value of actual data.
- **Item 4 (CSV):** missing columns that a customs manager or an HMRC return will need: **country of origin** (separate from installation country), **8-digit CN code**, **net mass**, **precursors** and their emissions, **electricity source and factor** (for indirect emissions), and the **carbon price relief evidence**. The Desk page refers to a "verifier-completed form HMRC needs from each producing installation". Recommend: "Send the EU communication template if you have it, **plus** these UK fields." The EU template alone is not enough for the UK relief claim.
- **Item 5:** the draft gives "UK ETS auction results (GOV.UK / ICE Endex)". The panel's understanding is that UK ETS auctions are held on **ICE Futures Europe**, while EEX runs EU primary auctions. *Verify before build.* Do not show a projected next-quarter rate without a clear "illustration, not a forecast" label.

## Part G. How to pitch the public-affairs material (items 31–35)

1. **Separate the hats.** Present the Bills, consultation responses and monitors as **independent policy work**, on a "Policy and law reform" page apart from the paid services. Each page should say: *not commissioned or funded by any client*, if true. That protects his credibility with officials, and his position for public appointments, where the Nolan principles and declarations of political activity apply.
2. **Know the lobbying rules before selling public affairs.** If Robert communicates with UK ministers or permanent secretaries **on behalf of paying clients**, the consultant lobbying register under the Transparency of Lobbying, Non-Party Campaigning and Trade Union Administration Act 2014 may apply *[verify the current scope]*. NI has no statutory register, but the CIPR Code of Conduct applies to him as a member. One line on the services page ("I follow the CIPR Code; I will tell you when registration applies") signals professionalism.
3. **Lead with the problem and the evidence, not the Bill.** For officials and MLAs, the dormant-law register and the *law on the books* piece are more persuasive than clause counts. Publish findings, keep the full Bill text back until the Government and Official Opposition audiences have had it, and say so. That courtesy is itself a signal.
4. **Be cross-party in form as well as substance.** Same briefing to Government and Opposition, dated, with office-holders checked on the day. In NI, brief all main parties, not a selection, before May 2027. Avoid question headlines that imply a side (item 33).
5. **Close the loop on consultation responses.** A short standard format for each published response: the question, his answer, his evidence, his proposed wording, and later **what happened** (the government's response and whether his point moved anything). An outcome log is what turns "I respond to consultations" into public-affairs standing.
6. **Build the bridge to clients.** Every public-affairs page should end with one line for firms ("What this means if you make or import goods in NI") and a link to the relevant service. Trade bodies and councils are the paying public-affairs clients; offer them a "consultation response support" service at a fixed price taken from the live site.
7. **Get the devolution and Brussels detail exactly right.** NI's edge is that it sits in two rulebooks. Every public-affairs item should name the Windsor Framework route (Joint Committee, Specialised Committee, Assembly scrutiny, Stormont Brake) where relevant, and the Sewel convention for UK Bills. Mistakes here (item 35's UK ETS attribution, item 31's Law Commission line) cost more standing than any other kind of error.
