Ask why sulphur hexafluoride (SF₆) counts towards a “carbon footprint” and you have a fair point: there is not a carbon atom in it. Nor in nitrous oxide (N₂O), nitrogen trifluoride (NF₃) or water. Yet a switchgear supplier, a fertiliser maker and a chip fab can all be told to report their “carbon”.

The answer is that “carbon” has become shorthand for “greenhouse gases, measured in carbon dioxide terms”. The more useful question is what makes a gas a greenhouse gas at all, and why some of them count thousands of times more than CO₂. That is chemistry, and it is worth getting right.

## Carbon is the currency, not the content

Carbon accounts convert every greenhouse gas into **carbon dioxide equivalent (CO₂e)** using its **global warming potential (GWP)**: how much heat one tonne of the gas traps over a set period, usually 100 years, compared with one tonne of CO₂.

So one tonne of SF₆ is reported as about 24,300 tonnes of CO₂e. It does not mean the gas contains carbon. It means it does the same warming damage as that much CO₂ would.

International reporting has covered a fixed “basket” of gases since the Kyoto Protocol. Nitrogen trifluoride joined it for 2013–2020 under the Doha Amendment, making seven:

| Gas | Contains carbon? | Where it comes from |
|---|---|---|
| Carbon dioxide (CO₂) | Yes | Burning fuel, cement, land clearing |
| Methane (CH₄) | Yes | Livestock, landfill, gas leaks, rice, wetlands |
| Nitrous oxide (N₂O) | No | Fertiliser and manure on soil, nitric and adipic acid plants |
| Hydrofluorocarbons (HFCs) | Yes | Fridges, air conditioning, heat pumps, aerosols |
| Perfluorocarbons (PFCs) | Yes | Aluminium smelting, electronics |
| Sulphur hexafluoride (SF₆) | No | Electrical switchgear, magnesium casting |
| Nitrogen trifluoride (NF₃) | No | Making semiconductors and flat screens |

Water vapour is not on the list. More on why below.

## How much each one counts

The figures below are from the IPCC's latest assessment (AR6, 2021). The UK still reports on the previous set (AR5, 2014): the UK greenhouse gas inventory, the Northern Ireland inventory published on 9 June 2026, and the DESNZ conversion factors for 2025 and 2026 all use AR5, because the UN rules have not yet moved on. Check which set your figures use before comparing them.

| Gas | Lifetime in the air | GWP over 20 years (AR6) | GWP over 100 years (AR6) | GWP over 100 years (AR5, used in UK reporting) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Carbon dioxide | Centuries to millennia (no single figure) | 1 | 1 | 1 |
| Methane | 11.8 years | 81.2 | 27.9 | 28 |
| Nitrous oxide | 109 years | 273 | 273 | 265 |
| HFC-32 | 5.4 years | 2,690 | 771 | 677 |
| HFC-134a | 14 years | 4,140 | 1,530 | 1,300 |
| CF₄ (a PFC) | 50,000 years | 5,300 | 7,380 | 6,630 |
| C₂F₆ (a PFC) | 10,000 years | 8,940 | 12,400 | 11,100 |
| NF₃ | 569 years | 13,400 | 17,400 | 16,100 |
| SF₆ | 1,000 years | 18,200 | 24,300 | 23,500 |
| Water vapour | About 9 days | Not given | Not given | Not given |

Two things jump out. **Short-lived gases look worse over 20 years:** methane traps about 81 times as much heat as CO₂ in its first 20 years, but it is mostly gone after a few decades, so the 100-year figure is 28. **Long-lived gases look the same or worse over 100 years,** because they are still there doing damage long after the methane has gone. Choosing 20 or 100 years is a policy choice, not a scientific fact; most rules use 100.

AR6 also cut SF₆'s lifetime from 3,200 years to 1,000 after new measurements of how it is destroyed in the upper atmosphere. Studies since put it somewhere between about 850 and 1,300 years. Still a very long time.

## What makes a gas a greenhouse gas

The Earth is warmed by sunlight and cools by glowing in the infrared, mostly at wavelengths between about 5 and 30 micrometres (µm), peaking near 10 µm. A greenhouse gas is one that absorbs some of that glow on the way out.

A molecule can only absorb infrared if one of its vibrations **changes its electrical balance**, its dipole. That is the whole rule.

- **Nitrogen and oxygen** make up 99% of dry air but are two identical atoms joined together. However they stretch, they stay balanced, so they ignore the Earth's glow. They are not greenhouse gases.
- **Water** is bent, with its hydrogens at 104.5°, so it has a dipole all the time. It absorbs across a wide range.
- **Carbon dioxide** is straight and balanced at rest, but when it bends or stretches unevenly it becomes lopsided for a moment, and that is enough.

## Where each gas catches the heat

Every bond vibrates at its own frequency, so each gas absorbs at its own set of wavelengths. A molecule emits at the same wavelengths it absorbs, which matters later.

| Gas | Main absorption bands | What is vibrating |
|---|---|---|
| Water vapour | 2.7 µm and 6.3 µm, plus a broad rotational band from about 18 µm out into the microwave | O–H stretches and the H–O–H bend; at long wavelengths the whole molecule spinning |
| Carbon dioxide | 15 µm (667 cm⁻¹) and 4.3 µm (2,349 cm⁻¹) | The bend, and the uneven stretch |
| Methane | 3.3 µm and 7.7 µm | C–H stretches and bends |
| Nitrous oxide | 4.5, 7.8 and 17 µm | N–N and N–O stretches, and the bend |
| Ozone | 9.6 µm | The O–O–O stretch |
| CF₄ and C₂F₆ (PFCs) | About 7.8–9.4 µm | C–F stretches |
| SF₆ | 10.5 µm (948 cm⁻¹) | S–F stretch |
| NF₃ | About 10.4–11.9 µm | N–F stretches |

This is why the fluorinated gases are so potent. Water vapour and CO₂ between them already absorb most of the Earth's glow at their own wavelengths, so adding a little more of either closes a gap that is already mostly shut. But between about 8 and 14 µm there is a gap, the **atmospheric window**, where heat escapes to space almost freely. Carbon–fluorine, sulphur–fluorine and nitrogen–fluorine bonds absorb strongly right in that window. A few molecules of SF₆ block heat that nothing else was blocking.

Add a lifetime of hundreds or thousands of years, and you get GWPs in the thousands.

### Absorb, shake, pass it on

What happens after a molecule absorbs? It is set vibrating: an **excited** state, in chemist's terms, but not ionised or broken. In the lower atmosphere it almost always bumps into a nitrogen or oxygen molecule within a fraction of a second and hands the energy over as motion, which is heat. That is how a gas making up 0.04% of the air warms the other 99.96%.

The reverse also happens: collisions set greenhouse molecules vibrating, and they emit infrared in all directions, some back down. High up, where the air is thin and cold, they emit less, so less heat leaves. Adding gas pushes the level from which heat escapes higher and colder, and the surface warms until the books balance again.

### Water and microwaves

Water does absorb microwaves, but not quite in the way the kitchen myth says. Water **vapour** has rotational lines at 22.235 GHz and 183.31 GHz, and weather satellites listen at those frequencies to measure humidity through cloud. A microwave oven works at 2.45 GHz, which is not a water resonance. It heats food by **dielectric heating**: the electric field keeps twisting liquid water molecules back and forth, and the jostling becomes heat. The 2.45 GHz frequency was chosen because it was free to use, not because water likes it.

## Where each gas meets its end

Every gas is eventually destroyed or removed. Where and how decides its lifetime, and so its GWP.

<figure class="learn-figure"><a href="{{ '/assets/learn/gas-fates.svg' | relative_url }}" title="Open the diagram full size"><img src="{{ '/assets/learn/gas-fates.svg' | relative_url }}" alt="Water vapour rains out of the lower atmosphere in about nine days. HFC-134a (14 years) and methane (11.8 years) are broken down in the troposphere by the hydroxyl radical, which pulls off a hydrogen atom; methane ends up as carbon dioxide and water. Nitrous oxide (109 years) and nitrogen trifluoride (569 years) rise to the stratosphere and are split by ultraviolet light and excited oxygen atoms; some nitrous oxide becomes nitric oxide, which destroys ozone. Sulphur hexafluoride (about 1,000 years) is destroyed only in the mesosphere by free electrons and hard ultraviolet. CF4 (50,000 years) survives almost everything." loading="lazy"></a><figcaption>The higher a gas must climb before something can break it, the longer it lasts. **Water vapour: rained out.** A water molecule spends about nine days in the air on average before it condenses and falls. Nothing chemical needed.

**Methane and HFCs: the atmosphere's detergent.** In the lower atmosphere, sunlight makes small amounts of the **hydroxyl radical**, OH. It does not add hydrogen to methane (that would be hydrogenation); it does the opposite and pulls a hydrogen atom off, leaving water and a methyl radical. That sets off a chain through formaldehyde and carbon monoxide that ends as **CO₂ and water**. So fossil methane, once it is gone, leaves its carbon behind as CO₂. HFCs were designed with hydrogen in them for the same reason: it gives OH something to grab, so they break down in years, not millennia.

**Nitrous oxide: up to the ozone layer.** N₂O is too stable for OH. It drifts up into the stratosphere, where about 90% is split by ultraviolet light into nitrogen and an excited oxygen atom. Most of the rest reacts with those excited oxygen atoms, and some of that makes **nitric oxide (NO)**, which destroys ozone in a catalytic cycle. Since the CFCs were phased out, N₂O has been the single biggest ozone-depleting emission. It is both a greenhouse gas and an ozone-layer problem, and farming is the main human source.

**NF₃: stratospheric ultraviolet.** Similar route to N₂O, slower: split by ultraviolet and excited oxygen in the stratosphere, over centuries.

**SF₆: the mesosphere.** Almost nothing in the lower atmosphere touches it. It is destroyed above about 50 km, mainly when it **captures a free electron** to become a negative ion (SF₆⁻), which then falls apart, and by the hardest ultraviolet (Lyman-alpha). This is the one gas on the list where ionisation really is the exit route, and it is why its lifetime was so hard to pin down.

**PFCs: almost forever.** CF₄ is among the most stable molecules made by people. It survives everything below the thermosphere; the main way to destroy it is in a furnace hot enough to break carbon–fluorine bonds. With a 50,000-year lifetime, about two-thirds of the CF₄ released from an aluminium smelter today will still be in the air 20,000 years from now.

**Carbon dioxide: no single exit.** CO₂ is not destroyed in the air at all. It is swapped into the oceans, plants and soils over years to centuries, and the last part is removed by rock weathering over many thousands of years. That is why it has no single lifetime in the table.

## Why water vapour is not counted

Water vapour is the biggest greenhouse gas by effect. It is left out of carbon accounts because people do not control how much is in the air; temperature does. Warm air holds more vapour (about 7% more per degree Celsius), and any extra we add rains out within days.

So water vapour is a **feedback, not a driver**. When CO₂ warms the air, the air holds more water, which traps more heat. NASA describes increased water vapour as a consequence of warming, not a cause; the Met Office says it is not a driver of today's warming. It amplifies roughly whatever the other gases start.

The exceptions are where water is put somewhere it would not otherwise be, such as aircraft vapour trails high up, or methane breaking down into water in the stratosphere. Those effects are studied separately.

## Why this matters to a business

- **Fertiliser:** a lot of the footprint of nitrogen fertiliser is N₂O, from making nitric acid and from spreading it on fields. Both CBAMs count N₂O from nitric acid production in the embedded emissions of fertiliser goods.
- **Aluminium:** smelters emit CF₄ and C₂F₆ during “anode effects”. Both CBAMs count PFCs for aluminium goods. A small leak, multiplied by a GWP of 7,000 to 12,000, matters.
- **Switchgear and electronics:** SF₆ insulates high-voltage switchgear and NF₃ cleans chip-making equipment. A leak of a few kilograms of SF₆ is tens of tonnes of CO₂e.
- **Cooling:** HFC leaks from fridges, air conditioning and heat pumps go straight into a Scope 1 footprint. Lower-GWP refrigerants change the numbers more than most efficiency projects.
- **Reporting:** say which GWP set you used (AR5 for UK reporting today), and whether you used 20 or 100 years. A methane-heavy footprint can look three times bigger on a 20-year basis.

## Common mistakes

1. **“Carbon” means CO₂ only.** In most footprints it means all seven gases in CO₂e. Ask.
2. **Mixing GWP sets.** An AR6 figure for one supplier and an AR5 figure for another will not add up.
3. **Treating water vapour as an emission to cut.** It is a feedback; cut the gases that drive it.
4. **Forgetting what methane becomes.** It breaks down into CO₂; fossil methane leaves extra CO₂ behind.
5. **Thinking microwave ovens prove water's resonance.** Satellites use water vapour's lines; ovens use dielectric heating.
