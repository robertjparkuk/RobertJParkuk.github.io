Most new homes in Northern Ireland are built the way they have been for decades: two leaves of concrete block, a cavity, insulation and a coat of render. It works, it is familiar and every builder knows how to do it. It also carries a carbon cost that is paid in full on the first day, long before the heating is switched on.

This page looks at where that carbon comes from, how block, brick, lime, timber and factory-built homes compare, and why the answer is less tidy than the brochures suggest. It builds on [Embodied or embedded carbon?]({{ '/learn/embodied-vs-embedded-carbon/' | relative_url }}).

## In short

| | Concrete block | Fired clay brick | Lime | Timber frame | “Prefab” (MMC) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Where the CO₂ comes from** | Cement: limestone broken down in the kiln, plus fuel | Fuel for the kiln, mostly gas | Limestone broken down, plus fuel | Sawmilling, drying, transport | Depends on the material used |
| **Takes CO₂ back?** | A little, slowly | No | Air lime, partly, over years | Stores carbon while the wood lasts | Depends |
| **Reusable?** | Rarely, if laid in cement mortar | Yes, if laid in lime mortar | Mortar lets bricks be reused | Yes, if designed for it | Depends on how it is joined |
| **Lifetime** | Long | Centuries | Long, needs care | Long if kept dry | Depends |

## Where the CO₂ comes from

<figure class="learn-figure"><a href="{{ '/assets/learn/cement-lime-brick.svg' | relative_url }}" title="Open the diagram full size"><img src="{{ '/assets/learn/cement-lime-brick.svg' | relative_url }}" alt="Three panels. Portland cement: limestone and clay heated to about 1,450 degrees; calcium carbonate gives calcium oxide and CO2; the oxide reacts with clay to make clinker, ground with gypsum into cement. CO2 comes from the stone itself plus fuel for the hottest kiln. Lime: limestone heated to about 900 degrees, then slaked to calcium hydroxide; process CO2 plus fuel; air lime sets by taking CO2 back. Fired clay brick: clay fired at about 1,000 degrees; no limestone, so almost all its CO2 comes from fuel." loading="lazy"></a><figcaption>Two sources of CO₂ in every kiln: the fuel, and (for cement and lime) the rock. Tap or click to open it full size.</figcaption></figure>

**Cement** is the glue in concrete block, mortar and render. Limestone (calcium carbonate) and clay are heated to about 1,450 °C. The heat drives CO₂ out of the stone:

**CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂**

The calcium oxide then reacts with the clay to make **clinker**, which is ground with a little gypsum into cement. The IPCC's default is **0.52 kg of CO₂ from the rock for every kg of clinker**, before any fuel is counted. No change of fuel can remove that part; only less clinker, or carbon capture, can. The IPCC's older review put cement at about 5% of world CO₂, roughly half from the rock and half from energy.

**Lime** comes from the same reaction at a lower temperature, about 900 °C. Per kilogram it releases more process CO₂ than clinker, because it is nearly pure calcium oxide: the IPCC default is **0.75 kg CO₂ per kg of high-calcium lime** and 0.59 kg for hydraulic lime, plus the fuel.

**Fired clay brick** contains no limestone. Clay is shaped, dried and fired at over 1,000 °C (Brick Development Association, a trade body), so its CO₂ comes almost entirely from the fuel, mostly natural gas.

## The lime cycle: back to stone

<figure class="learn-figure"><a href="{{ '/assets/learn/lime-cycle.svg' | relative_url }}" title="Open the diagram full size"><img src="{{ '/assets/learn/lime-cycle.svg' | relative_url }}" alt="A four-step loop. Limestone is quarried, burned in a kiln at about 900 degrees giving quicklime and CO2, slaked with water to give calcium hydroxide, and as lime mortar sets in air it slowly takes CO2 back and turns into calcium carbonate again. The process CO2 is partly reabsorbed over years; the fuel burned to heat the kiln is not." loading="lazy"></a><figcaption>Lime goes back to the stone it came from. Tap or click to open it full size.</figcaption></figure>

Quicklime is slaked with water to make lime putty:

**CaO + H₂O → Ca(OH)₂**

**Air lime** (non-hydraulic) then sets by taking CO₂ back out of the air:

**Ca(OH)₂ + CO₂ → CaCO₃ + H₂O**

It is a genuine loop, but not a perfect one. The fuel used to heat the kiln is never paid back. Carbonation is slow and stops where air cannot reach, and **hydraulic limes** set partly with water, as cement does, so they take back less. One modelling study of hempcrete found the lime binder reabsorbed only **18.5–38.4%** of the CO₂ released in making it (*Journal of Cleaner Production*, 2020). Cement and concrete also reabsorb a little over their lives, but only a fraction.

The bigger benefit of lime is practical. **Lime mortar is softer than the brick**, so a lime-mortared wall can be taken down and the bricks cleaned and used again. Research reviewed by Zhou and colleagues (2020) found about 85% of bricks laid in lime mortar could be separated cleanly; cement mortar, used widely from the 1920s, makes that far harder. A Finnish study published in March 2026 found reclaimed bricks carried 86–95% less carbon than new ones.

## Kilogram for kilogram, and house for house

<figure class="learn-figure"><a href="{{ '/assets/learn/wall-material-factors.svg' | relative_url }}" title="Open the chart full size"><img src="{{ '/assets/learn/wall-material-factors.svg' | relative_url }}" alt="Cradle-to-gate factors in kilograms of CO2 equivalent per kilogram: fired clay brick 0.21; softwood studs 0.26 excluding stored carbon; lightweight concrete block 0.28; cross-laminated timber 0.25 to 0.44; lime 0.75 for the process CO2 alone, plus fuel; world-average steel sections 1.55. Per kilogram comparisons mislead because walls use very different weights of each material." loading="lazy"></a><figcaption>Per kilogram, block and brick look cheap. But a block wall weighs far more. Tap or click to open it full size.</figcaption></figure>

The usual source for these factors is the **ICE database** (Inventory of Carbon and Energy), started at the University of Bath and now run by Circular Ecology. The figures above are from version 3 (2019) as published by the Institution of Structural Engineers in 2020. ICE has since reached version 5 (June 2026), so check the current release before using a number in a real assessment.

Per kilogram, brick and block look much the same as timber. That comparison misleads, because a masonry wall uses several times the weight of material. The fair test is per square metre of wall, or of floor, for walls built to the same insulation standard.

<figure class="learn-figure"><a href="{{ '/assets/learn/house-embodied-by-wall.svg' | relative_url }}" title="Open the chart full size"><img src="{{ '/assets/learn/house-embodied-by-wall.svg' | relative_url }}" alt="Embodied carbon of an 84 square metre social home in Ireland by wall type: brick-clad cavity 526 kg CO2e per square metre of floor; rendered block cavity 496; 215 mm single-leaf block with external insulation 492; timber frame with cellulose insulation, insulated foundations and 50% GGBS cement replacement 369." loading="lazy"></a><figcaption>Same house, same energy standard, different structure. Tap or click to open it full size.</figcaption></figure>

An AECB study for the Passive House Association of Ireland, reported in August 2021, modelled the same 84 m² social home four ways (the report seen does not say exactly which life-cycle stages were counted). The three masonry versions came out within 7% of each other at about **500 kg CO₂e per m² of floor**; the timber-frame version, which also used insulated foundations and less cement in the concrete, was about **370**. A separate pair of Irish houses modelled in 2021 gave 353 against 218 kg CO₂e per m². Both studies are small, but the direction matches a 2024 policy study for Essex councils: timber structure with natural insulation cut upfront carbon in low-rise homes by about 40%, and the biggest single items in every home were **concrete, bricks, blocks, plasterboard and metals**. The substructure and structure together (foundations, slabs, frame and walls) often account for over half of the upfront carbon, and foundations and slabs are concrete whatever the walls are made of.

## Hempcrete: “carbon negative”?

Hempcrete is hemp shiv (the woody core of the stalk) mixed with a lime binder. The hemp took CO₂ from the air as it grew, and the lime takes some back as it sets, so it is often sold as carbon negative.

The evidence says: **sometimes**. A 2020 study of 36 mixes found the best came out at about **−16 kg CO₂e per m² of wall**, but denser mixes, and any with Portland cement, were net emitters. A 2024 US study found hempcrete was carbon negative only when hemp made up at least 20% of the weight and the lime's carbonation was counted. Counting the stored plant carbon also depends on the accounting rules (EN 15804+A2), and on the wall lasting.

## “Prefab” and modern methods

**Modern methods of construction (MMC)** is the official term. The government's 2019 framework sorts it into seven categories, from whole volumetric modules (category 1) and flat-pack panels (category 2) to site-based productivity gains (category 7).

MMC changes **where** a house is built, not **what** it is made of. A factory-built steel or concrete module can carry as much carbon as a block house; a timber-panel system usually carries less because it is timber. Factory building can cut waste and improve quality, but no general figure for those savings was verified for this page.

It has also been a hard business. House by Urban Splash went into administration in May 2022; Legal & General paused its modular homes factory in spring 2023; and Ilke Homes went into administration at the end of June 2023 with net debts of over £319 million, including about £68 million owed to Homes England.

**Thermal mass** is often given as the reason heavy walls pay back over time. An NHBC Foundation study by BRE (2011) found it made little difference to UK homes: the largest overall CO₂ difference between masonry and timber-frame houses over 60 years was 4%. As homes get better insulated and the grid gets cleaner, **embodied carbon becomes the larger share** of a new home's lifetime footprint (my reasoning from that study, not a sourced figure).

## Why it matters in Northern Ireland

- **Block and render is the default.** That NI new homes are mostly cavity block was not confirmed in an official statistic for this page, but it is what most builders and buyers see.
- **NI makes its own cement.** Cookstown (about 0.5 million tonnes a year, sold by Holcim in January 2022) and Mannok at Derrylin, Co Fermanagh (trade press). Local cement is not low-carbon cement; ask for each product's EPD.
- **A large building programme.** The Executive's housing supply strategy aims for 100,000 homes by 2039, a third of them social. The Housing Executive's first new homes in about 25 years, six semi-detached houses at Sunningdale Gardens in north Belfast, were built using modern methods of construction to Passivhaus standard, and in May 2025 it sought a feasibility study comparing MMC with traditional masonry, including carbon.
- **Energy rules, not carbon rules.** The Part F uplift came into force on **30 June 2022** and governs how much energy a new home uses. There is no NI rule yet on the carbon built into it.
- **Climate targets.** The Climate Change Act (Northern Ireland) 2022 sets net zero by 2050 and at least 48% below the baseline by 2030. Construction materials are counted mostly as someone else's emissions (the cement plant's, or another country's), which is why the [embodied carbon]({{ '/learn/embodied-vs-embedded-carbon/' | relative_url }}) of a council or housing association's buildings sits in its Scope 3.

### Think of a cake

| Baking a cake | Building a wall |
|---|---|
| The oven's gas bill | Fuel for the kiln |
| Baking powder giving off gas as it rises | Limestone giving off CO₂ as it turns to lime |
| A cake that is mostly flour | A wall that is mostly block: heavy, so the total adds up |
| A lighter sponge | Timber frame: less material for the same job |
| Icing that peels off cleanly | Lime mortar: the bricks can be used again |
| Icing set like concrete | Cement mortar: the bricks usually go to hardcore |
| A cake from a factory | MMC: it is still the recipe that matters |

<p class="sector">Housing associations and councils</p>

### Choosing a structure for 40 social homes

A housing association is deciding between block cavity and timber frame for a scheme of 40 two-storey homes of about 84 m².

| | Rendered block cavity | Timber frame |
|---|---|---|
| Embodied carbon (AECB study, illustrative) | About 496 kg CO₂e per m² | About 369 kg CO₂e per m² |
| Per home (84 m²) | About 41,700 kg | About 31,000 kg |
| Scheme of 40 | About 1,670 tonnes | About 1,240 tonnes |

**The lesson:** on these figures, timber frame saves about 430 tonnes across the scheme (my arithmetic, from a single study). Ask for a whole-life assessment of your own design and check the foundations and slabs, which are a large item. These numbers are an illustration, not a design figure.
</div>

<div class="example" markdown="1">
<p class="sector">Heritage and repair</p>

### Repointing an old stone or brick building

A council owns a pre-1920 building laid in lime mortar and has a quote to repoint it in cement.

**What to know:** lime mortar is softer and more breathable than the stone or brick, and lets the units be reused later. Cement mortar is harder than old bricks, traps moisture and can make the bricks crack instead of the joints.

**The lesson:** repair like with like. Using lime is a conservation choice first, but it also keeps the bricks reusable at the end of the building's life.
</div>

<div class="example" markdown="1">
<p class="sector">Manufacturing</p>

### A block or precast maker selling to public clients

A County Tyrone block maker is asked by a council contractor for the product's carbon footprint.

**What to do:** commission an **Environmental Product Declaration (EPD)** to EN 15804, which gives the A1–A3 figure in kg CO₂e per tonne or per m³ (or per block). Most of the result will be the cement, so the main levers are less clinker (using GGBS or other cement replacements, where supply allows) and a lower-carbon cement supplier.

**The lesson:** public clients are starting to ask. A verified EPD lets the product compete on numbers rather than claims.
</div>

## Common mistakes

1. **Comparing per kilogram.** Compare per m² of wall or floor at the same insulation standard.
2. **“Lime is carbon neutral.”** It takes back part of its process CO₂, slowly. The fuel is never repaid.
3. **“Prefab is low-carbon.”** It depends on the material, not on the factory.
4. **“Hempcrete is always carbon negative.”** Only some mixes, counted in a particular way.
5. **Forgetting the foundations.** Concrete slabs and footings are a large item, whatever the walls are made of.
6. **Counting the timber's stored carbon as a saving at the gate.** Report it separately, and only if the timber is sustainably sourced and the building lasts.
