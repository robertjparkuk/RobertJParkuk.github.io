Walk past a fish processor in Kilkeel or Portavogie and you will see the problem: for every box of fillets or prawn tails, there is a bin of heads, frames, guts and shell. Some of it goes to rendering or fishmeal; some of it costs money to get rid of.

The same material is valuable elsewhere. Fish oil, collagen for cosmetics, chitosan for water treatment, calcium for hens and lime for fields can all come from the bits the customer never sees. This page follows those by-products through the biological loop of the [circular economy]({{ '/learn/circular-economy-life-cycle/' | relative_url }}), and asks a sharper question: is a seashell a carbon store?

## In short

| Part | Share | What it can become | The catch |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Fillet** | About a third of cod, haddock or whiting; about half of herring or mackerel | Food | Yields vary widely |
| **Heads and frames** | Most of the rest | Fishmeal, fish oil, stock, protein | Needs collection and a plant |
| **Guts (viscera)** | About 5% of a fish | Oil, enzymes, silage, biogas | Spoils fast |
| **Skin and scales** | Varies | Collagen, gelatine, leather | Needs clean handling |
| **Mollusc shell** | Up to 75% of a mussel or oyster | Lime, poultry grit, aggregate, filters | Heavy; transport eats the benefit |
| **Prawn and crab shell** | Most of the waste at a prawn plant | Chitin and chitosan, oil, pigment | Processing uses acid, alkali and heat |

The UN Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO) puts fish processing by-products at **30–70% of the whole fish**. They already matter: in 2022 by-products made up **34% of the world's fishmeal and 53% of its fish oil** (FAO, *State of World Fisheries and Aquaculture 2024*).

- **Fish oil** is concentrated in the liver and gut area.
- **Collagen and gelatine** come mainly from skin and scales.
- **Viscera** are a cheap source of enzymes, and can be made into protein hydrolysates for feed or fertiliser (Pelagia in Shetland began making the UK's first liquid fish hydrolysate from salmon-farm by-products in 2019, according to Seafish).
- **Silage**: minced fish kept with a little acid, so its own enzymes turn it to a stable liquid feed.
- **Anaerobic digestion** turns what is left into biogas; a 2025 review of 39 studies found yields vary widely, and mixing fish waste with other feedstocks often helps.

## The chemistry of shells

<figure class="learn-figure"><a href="{{ '/assets/learn/shell-chemistry.svg' | relative_url }}" title="Open the diagram full size"><img src="{{ '/assets/learn/shell-chemistry.svg' | relative_url }}" alt="Four panels. Building a shell in the sea: calcium plus two bicarbonate ions give calcium carbonate, CO2 and water, so calcification releases CO2, about 0.6 per carbonate in seawater at 25 degrees. Burning shells for lime at about 900 degrees: calcium carbonate gives calcium oxide and CO2, 0.44 kilograms of CO2 per kilogram of carbonate plus kiln fuel, the same as limestone. Crushing shells unburned for liming, grit or aggregate: on acid soil carbonate neutralises acid and releases CO2, replacing quarried limestone that would release the same. Tipping shells, where allowed: the carbonate stays put, but attached flesh rots, it costs money, and a usable material is lost." loading="lazy"></a><figcaption>Shell is carbonate, but carbonate is not automatically a carbon store. Tap or click to open it full size.</figcaption></figure>

Mussel and oyster shells are **95–99.9% calcium carbonate**, with a little protein holding the crystals together (Morris and colleagues, 2019). Oysters build mostly calcite; some clams build aragonite, a different crystal form of the same compound.

**Building a shell releases CO₂.** In seawater, carbon is mostly held as bicarbonate. To make a shell, a mussel combines calcium with bicarbonate:

**Ca²⁺ + 2HCO₃⁻ → CaCO₃ + CO₂ + H₂O**

Seawater's chemistry soaks some of that up again, but not all: about **0.6 CO₂ is released for each unit of carbonate** at 25 °C, and more in colder water (Frankignoulle and colleagues, 1994). On balance, growing shells pushes CO₂ towards the air, not away from it. A 2025 review by Pernet and colleagues, *Cracking the myth: bivalve farming is not a CO₂ sink*, concluded that the evidence does not support treating mussel or oyster farming as a carbon sink, and that field measurements are needed before any carbon credits are claimed. (A rebuttal has been published; the debate is not over.)

**Burning shells releases the rest.** Shell can replace limestone as a source of lime, but it is the same reaction:

**CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂**

That is **0.44 kg of CO₂ for every kg of carbonate** (arithmetic from the formula weights, 44 ÷ 100), plus the fuel for the kiln. Shell lime is recycled lime, not carbon-free lime.

**Crushed, unburned shell** is often the better use. It can replace quarried limestone for liming acid soils, as calcium grit for laying hens (where crushed oyster shell performs as well as limestone), as aggregate, or as a filter that captures phosphate and metals. One study cited by Morris found no benefit over landfill once the shell had to travel more than about 320 km, so it works best close to where it is produced.

Scientists are now testing the reverse: Goossens and colleagues (*Biogeosciences*, 2026) buried crushed mussel shell in seabed mud to see whether dissolving it could cancel the CO₂ released when it grew. About 6% dissolved in 24 weeks. That is research, not a market.

## From prawn shell to chitosan

<figure class="learn-figure"><a href="{{ '/assets/learn/chitin-to-chitosan.svg' | relative_url }}" title="Open the diagram full size"><img src="{{ '/assets/learn/chitin-to-chitosan.svg' | relative_url }}" alt="A five-step process. Prawn and crab shell is chitin, protein and calcium carbonate. Dilute hydrochloric acid dissolves the carbonate, releasing CO2. Dilute sodium hydroxide or enzymes remove the protein. What is left is chitin, a sugar polymer like cellulose. Hot concentrated sodium hydroxide removes the acetyl groups to give chitosan, which binds fats, dyes, metals and fine particles, and is used in water treatment, wound dressings, food coatings, cosmetics and seed treatments." loading="lazy"></a><figcaption>Chitosan is the high-value route for crustacean shell. Tap or click to open it full size.</figcaption></figure>

Crustacean shell is a composite: about **20–30% chitin** and **30–50% calcium carbonate**, with protein, fat and pigment. **Chitin** is the second most common natural polymer after cellulose, and looks like it, except that each sugar unit carries an acetyl-amino group (–NHCOCH₃).

1. **Demineralise:** dilute hydrochloric acid dissolves the carbonate: **CaCO₃ + 2HCl → CaCl₂ + CO₂ + H₂O**.
2. **Deproteinise:** dilute sodium hydroxide, or enzymes, removes the protein.
3. **Deacetylate:** hot, concentrated sodium hydroxide (40–50%, around 100 °C, for hours) strips the acetyl groups, leaving amino groups (–NH₂). The product is **chitosan** (Younes and Rinaudo, 2015).

In mild acid those amino groups pick up a positive charge, so chitosan grabs negatively charged particles, oils, dyes and some metals. It is used in **water treatment**, wound dressings, food coatings and cosmetics. In Great Britain, CuanTec in Fife makes chitosan from Nephrops shell and Pennosan in Wales makes water-treatment products from crustacean shell (as reported by Seafish).

The chemicals and heat have their own footprint, so chitosan is only a good use of shell if the process is efficient. Check the process, not just the source.

## Why it matters in Northern Ireland

- **A shellfish fleet.** In 2024 the NI fleet landed **50,821 tonnes** of fish and shellfish worth **£63.1 million**; shellfish was 48.7% of the value. **Nephrops** (prawns, sold as scampi) is the single most valuable species: 6,279 tonnes from the western Irish Sea in 2024, from stocks the Assembly's research service says are declining (NI Assembly research paper, 15 April 2026, citing DAERA).
- **Tonnes of shell.** A Kilkeel processor told UTV in June 2024 that the town's factories produce about **1,000 tonnes of waste prawn shell a year** (a news report, not an official figure).
- **Shellfish farms.** Mussels and Pacific oysters in the sea loughs produced just over 3,000 tonnes worth over £4 million in 2018 (AFBI), and the shell is most of the weight.
- **Local research.** Kilkeel Seafoods, the NI Fish Producers' Organisation and Queen's University were reported in 2024 to be studying prawn-shell chitosan for removing phosphate from Lough Neagh (news reports; the study itself was not found). Teagasc's all-island AIMBio project, funded by the Shared Island Fund, has pilot sites including Rathlin Island. Queen's used 10 tonnes of clean scallop shell to help rebuild horse mussel reef in Strangford Lough in 2010.
- **Phosphate and Lough Neagh.** Shell and chitosan both bind phosphate, the nutrient behind the [Lough Neagh blooms]({{ '/learn/lough-neagh-and-cyanobacteria/' | relative_url }}). That is promising, but no product has been shown to work at the scale of the lough.

### Think of a Sunday roast

| A Sunday roast | Fish by-products |
|---|---|
| The joint on the table | The fillet |
| Bones boiled for stock | Frames for fishmeal, oil and stock |
| Dripping saved in a jar | Fish oil |
| Peelings to the compost bin | Guts to silage or biogas |
| Eggshells crushed for the garden | Crushed shell for liming or grit |
| Everything in the bin | The linear route: a cost, and lost value |
| Claiming the bin is a carbon store | Claiming a shell pile locks up carbon |

## Examples

<div class="example" markdown="1">
<p class="sector">Food and drink</p>

### A prawn processor with a shell problem

A County Down processor tails Nephrops and pays to get rid of the heads and shell.

**Options, in order of value:** sell or partner for **chitosan, oil or pigment** (the head waste holds oil, up to about 11.5% in summer in one study); supply a **protein and feed** processor; send it to **anaerobic digestion**; rendering as the last resort. All of it stays Category 3 animal by-product while flesh is attached, so it must move with the right paperwork to an approved plant.

**The lesson:** volume and steady supply decide which route works. Several processors together are a better partner for a chitosan plant than one.
</div>

<div class="example" markdown="1">
<p class="sector">Agriculture and food</p>

### A mussel farm and a tillage farm

A mussel farm has cleaned, crushed shell; a nearby farm buys ground limestone to lime its fields.

**What the chemistry says:** spread on acid soil, shell does the same job as limestone and releases the same CO₂ as it neutralises the acid. The gain is not a carbon store: it is **avoided quarrying, avoided waste disposal and short haulage**. Check first with DAERA whether shell with any flesh on it can go to land in NI, and how it must be treated.

**The lesson:** sell it as a local liming product, not as a carbon credit.
</div>

<div class="example" markdown="1">
<p class="sector">Public sector</p>

### A council with a harbour

A council owns a fishing harbour and is asked to back a by-products hub.

**What to weigh:** steady tonnages from local processors; an approved site under the animal by-products rules; markets for oil, protein, shell and chitosan; and the costs of transport. Councils can also buy the products, such as shell-based filter media or chitosan for water treatment, once they are proven.

**The lesson:** the business case comes from **collection at scale** and **local buyers**. Carbon is a co-benefit, counted honestly.
</div>

## Common mistakes

1. **“Shells lock up carbon.”** Making them released CO₂ in the sea; burning them releases the rest.
2. **“Shell lime is zero-carbon lime.”** It gives off the same 0.44 kg of CO₂ per kg of carbonate as limestone.
3. **“It is just waste.”** In law it is an animal by-product until the flesh is gone, with rules on how it moves.
4. **“Chitosan is always green.”** The acid, alkali and heat count too.
5. **“Mussel farms earn carbon credits.”** The evidence does not support it yet.
6. **Forgetting transport.** Shell is heavy; beyond a few hundred kilometres the benefit can vanish.
