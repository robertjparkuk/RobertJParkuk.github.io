Two words that sound the same, and are often used as if they were, mean different things depending on who is asking. A construction client, an EU customer and HMRC can each ask you for the "embodied" or "embedded" carbon in the same steel beam and expect three different numbers. All three can be right. The trouble starts when you send one of them the wrong one.

## Four meanings, side by side

| Term | Who uses it | What it covers | Set by |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Embodied carbon** | Architects, engineers, surveyors, construction clients | Everything in the life of a building or product **except** the energy and water used to run it: materials, manufacture, transport, construction, maintenance, replacement, demolition and disposal | RICS whole life carbon standard (2nd edition, in force from 1 July 2024), following BS EN 15978:2026 |
| **Embedded emissions** | The EU carbon border adjustment mechanism (CBAM) | Direct emissions from producing a good, plus emissions from the electricity used to make it, plus emissions in listed inputs ("precursors"). For iron and steel, aluminium, hydrogen and electricity, **direct emissions only** | Regulation (EU) 2023/956, Article 3(22) and Article 7, as amended by Regulation (EU) 2025/2083 |
| **Embodied emissions** | The UK CBAM, from 1 January 2027 | Direct emissions from producing a good, plus those in precursor goods. **No electricity** for any sector until 2029 at the earliest | Finance Act 2026, Part 5 (section 148 is headed "Embodied emissions"); CBAM (Emissions and Verification) Regulations 2026, SI 2026/995 |
| **Embedded (or embodied) emissions in trade** | Statisticians, Defra | Emissions released abroad to make the goods and services a country imports | Defra, *Carbon footprint for the UK and England to 2023* |

The last row is why the words get muddled. Defra's carbon footprint statistics (published 30 June 2026) say "embedded": in 2023, emissions from imports were 371 million tonnes of CO2e, 53% of the UK's 699 million tonne footprint. The methods note for the same statistics says "embodied". Government uses both words for the same idea; the law and the standards do not.

## What the carbon border taxes actually count

The two CBAMs look alike but draw the line in different places. As at 17 November 2026:

- **Direct emissions** are those from the production processes themselves. Under the EU rules this includes heat and cooling used in production even if you buy it in from someone else (Article 3(21)). That is different from ordinary company carbon reporting, where bought-in heat is Scope 2.
- **Electricity.** The EU counts the emissions from electricity used in production only for **cement and fertilisers**. For iron and steel, aluminium, hydrogen and electricity it counts direct emissions only (Annex II). The UK CBAM counts **no** electricity emissions for any sector; HMRC says indirect emissions are "delayed until 2029 at the earliest".
- **Precursors.** Both count the emissions already in key inputs: the crude steel in a steel beam, the unwrought aluminium in foil, the clinker in cement. Since the 2025 amendments, the EU counts only precursors listed in its Annex I and made outside the EU.
- **Transport** to the customer is not counted by either. The EU Regulation only asks the Commission to consider adding it.

So the same bag of fertiliser carries two different figures: the EU counts the electricity used to make it and the UK does not.

## Why it matters in Northern Ireland

- **UK CBAM** applies across the whole UK, including Northern Ireland, from 1 January 2027, once covered imports pass, or are expected to pass, £50,000 (HMRC, updated 2 November 2026). Your supplier's figure must follow the UK system boundaries, not an EU one or a product footprint.
- **EU customers** of NI and GB firms will ask for EU-style embedded emissions so that they can use actual data rather than the EU's default values. How EU CBAM treats goods entering Northern Ireland under the Windsor Framework is a customs question: take specialist advice.
- **Construction clients** increasingly ask for embodied carbon, usually through an Environmental Product Declaration (EPD) made to BS EN 15804. That is a cradle-to-gate figure or more, and it is not a CBAM figure.
- **Building rules** do not yet require it. Northern Ireland's building regulations cover operational energy and carbon only (Technical Booklets F1 and F2, uplifted from 30 June 2022). In 2023 the Department of Finance said it had "no resource" to develop its own approach to embodied carbon and would follow the other administrations. I found no embodied carbon requirement in England's Building Regulations either (to be checked: the Government said in July 2026 that it was "taking time to consider the right approach").

## Examples

<div class="example" markdown="1">
<p class="sector">Manufacturing</p>

### A roll of aluminium foil

A converter in Northern Ireland imports aluminium foil (commodity code 7607) to make food trays.

| Question | What is counted |
|---|---|
| UK CBAM (from 2027) | Direct emissions from rolling the foil and from producing the unwrought aluminium it was rolled from (the precursor). No electricity. |
| EU CBAM (for an EU customer) | The same, because aluminium is in Annex II: direct emissions only. |
| Product carbon footprint, cradle to gate (ISO 14067) | All of the above, **plus** the electricity used to smelt and roll the aluminium, mining and refining the bauxite and alumina, and transport to the factory. |
| Embodied carbon of the finished tray | The footprint above, plus making the tray, delivering it, and what happens to it at the end of its life. |

For aluminium made from ore, the electricity used to smelt it is usually a large part of its footprint, and how large depends on how that electricity is generated. Both carbon border taxes leave it out. A supplier who sends you its product footprint as its "CBAM figure" will overstate it; one who sends a CBAM figure as its product footprint will understate it badly.

**The lesson:** always ask which boundary a number was calculated to.
</div>

<div class="example" markdown="1">
<p class="sector">Construction</p>

### A steel beam in a new warehouse

A contractor in Antrim builds a warehouse with imported structural steel.

- The **importer** reports UK CBAM embodied emissions from 2027: direct emissions from making the section and the crude steel it came from.
- The **steel maker's EPD** gives a cradle-to-gate figure (modules A1 to A3), which also counts the electricity used in its furnaces.
- The **design team's** whole life carbon assessment adds delivery to site (A4), erection (A5), any repainting or replacement over the building's life (B2 to B4) and demolition and disposal (C1 to C4). The benefit of recycling the steel afterwards goes in module D, reported separately, not netted off.

**The lesson:** three numbers, all correct, for one beam. Only the first has a tax bill attached.
</div>

<div class="example" markdown="1">
<p class="sector">Agriculture and food</p>

### A bag of fertiliser on a dairy farm

A merchant imports ammonium nitrate fertiliser and sells it to farms in County Antrim.

- **EU CBAM** counts direct emissions from making the ammonia and nitric acid **and** the electricity used to make them, because fertilisers are not in Annex II.
- **UK CBAM** counts direct emissions only, for every sector, until at least 2029. Same bag, smaller number.
- Neither counts what happens after the bag is spread: the nitrous oxide released from the soil. That appears in the farm's own emissions and in the carbon footprint of its milk, which is what a processor or supermarket will ask the farmer for.

**The lesson:** a carbon border tax measures the carbon in the bag, not the carbon from using it.
</div>

## Common mistakes

1. **Sending a product footprint as a CBAM figure,** or the other way round.
2. **Assuming the EU and UK CBAM figures are the same.** For cement and fertiliser they are not, until the UK adds electricity.
3. **Treating "embodied" as one thing.** In a building assessment it covers the whole life except operation; in UK CBAM law it covers production only.
4. **Netting off recycling credits.** Module D is reported separately in building assessments, not deducted from the total.
5. **Forgetting bought-in heat.** CBAM counts heat and cooling used in production as direct emissions, wherever they were made.
