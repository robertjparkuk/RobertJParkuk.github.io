Two ideas get muddled under the word “capture”.

- **Carbon capture and storage (CCS)** catches CO₂ from a power station, cement kiln or factory and stores it underground. It stops new CO₂ reaching the air. It **reduces** emissions; it does not remove any.
- **Carbon dioxide removal (CDR)** takes CO₂ that is already in the air and locks it away: in trees, soils, rock, the deep ground or the ocean. Only removal can make something genuinely **carbon negative**.

The IPCC is blunt about both: removal “cannot substitute for immediate and deep emissions reductions”. It is for the emissions that cannot be cut, and later for bringing CO₂ back down.

## Capture, use and removal side by side

| Term | What it means | Example | Reduces or removes? |
|---|---|---|---|
| **CCS** | Capture CO₂ from a source and store it underground | Amine scrubber on a cement kiln | Reduces |
| **CCUS** | CCS, but some CO₂ is used (in drinks, chemicals, building products) | CO₂ into concrete blocks | Reduces, if the CO₂ stays locked up |
| **BECCS** | CCS on a plant burning biomass | Wood-fired power station with capture | Removes, if the biomass is regrown and supply chain emissions are low |
| **DACCS** | Direct air capture with storage | Fans and sorbents pulling CO₂ from air | Removes |
| **Biochar** | Plant waste heated without air into charcoal, dug into soil | Biochar from sawmill waste | Removes |
| **Enhanced rock weathering** | Crushed silicate rock spread on fields to react with CO₂ | Basalt dust on farmland | Removes |
| **Natural sinks** | Trees, soils, peat, seagrass, oceans | New woodland, rewetted bog | Removes, but can be reversed |
| **“Carbon negative”** | A claim that removals exceed emissions | A product or company label | A claim to test; not an IPCC term |

## How capture works: absorb or adsorb

There are two main ways to catch CO₂, and the words differ by one letter.

<figure class="learn-figure"><a href="{{ '/assets/learn/absorption-vs-adsorption.svg' | relative_url }}" title="Open the diagram full size"><img src="{{ '/assets/learn/absorption-vs-adsorption.svg' | relative_url }}" alt="Left, absorption: carbon dioxide dissolves into a liquid amine solvent at about 40 °C and reacts, 2 RNH2 plus CO2 giving a carbamate; heating to 100 to 120 °C releases pure CO2 for storage. Used in gas processing and power stations and now coming to cement, with a 10 to 40% energy penalty. Right, adsorption: carbon dioxide sticks to the inner surfaces of a porous solid such as a zeolite, a metal–organic framework or amine-coated silica, and is released by heat, vacuum or a pressure drop. Used in many direct air capture machines." loading="lazy"></a><figcaption>Absorb: into the whole liquid. Adsorb: onto a surface. Tap or click to open it full size.</figcaption></figure>

**Absorption** takes the gas into the bulk of a liquid, like a sponge. The workhorse is amine scrubbing, used to clean CO₂ out of natural gas since the 1930s and now being fitted to power stations and, from the end of this decade, cement kilns. Flue gas at about 40 °C bubbles through a solution of an amine such as monoethanolamine (MEA), and the CO₂ reacts:

**2 RNH₂ + CO₂ ⇌ RNHCOO⁻ + RNH₃⁺** (an amine carbamate)

The reaction runs backwards when heated. Steam strips the solvent at 100–120 °C, releasing a stream of nearly pure CO₂ to compress and pipe away, and the regenerated amine goes round again. That reheating is the cost: the IPCC put the energy penalty at 10–40% more energy than the same plant without capture, for capture of 85–95% of the CO₂.

**Adsorption** holds the gas on a surface, like Velcro. Porous solids such as zeolites, metal–organic frameworks or amine-coated silica have enormous internal surfaces; CO₂ sticks to them and is released by heat, vacuum or a drop in pressure. Many direct air capture machines work this way, because the CO₂ in air is so dilute (about 0.04%) that the sorbent must grab it at the surface.

### Turning CO₂ into stone

The most permanent store of all is rock. CO₂ dissolved in water is a weak acid, and it attacks calcium- and magnesium-rich rocks such as basalt and olivine to make carbonate minerals:

**Mg₂SiO₄ + 2CO₂ → 2MgCO₃ + SiO₂** (olivine to magnesite and silica)

**CaO + CO₂ → CaCO₃** (lime back to limestone)

Nature does this over millions of years. The CarbFix project in Iceland speeds it up by pumping CO₂ dissolved in water into basalt: over 95% had turned into carbonate minerals within two years. It is a little like petrification, where minerals slowly fill a buried log, except here the gas itself becomes the stone.

Captured CO2 pumped into basalt with water turns into carbonate rock in under two years, as at CarbFix in Iceland, and so back to stone." loading="lazy"></a><figcaption>Carbonate minerals made, broken and made again: shell, limestone, kiln, mortar, and back to rock. The same chemistry explains why **cement** is so hard to decarbonise. Heating limestone releases CO₂ from the rock itself, whatever fuel is used: **CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂**, about 0.52 kg of CO₂ for every kilogram of clinker before any fuel is burned (more per kilogram of lime, which is nearly pure CaO). Only capture can deal with that part. Mortar and concrete win a little back over the years as their calcium hydroxide re-absorbs CO₂: **Ca(OH)₂ + CO₂ → CaCO₃ + H₂O**.

## Nature's own capture

| Store | How it captures carbon | Reaction |
|---|---|---|
| Trees and plants | Photosynthesis | 6CO₂ + 6H₂O + light → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ |
| Soils | Roots and dead plants become soil organic matter | Slow, and easily lost by ploughing or drainage |
| Peat bogs | Waterlogging stops dead moss rotting | Carbon builds up over thousands of years |
| Seagrass and saltmarsh | Trap carbon-rich sediment | “Blue carbon” |
| Shells and corals | Lock carbon into calcium carbonate; forming it releases some CO₂ locally, so the long-term store is the limestone they leave | Ca²⁺ + 2HCO₃⁻ → CaCO₃ + CO₂ + H₂O |
| The ocean | Dissolves CO₂; plankton sink to the deep | The biological pump |

The scale is striking. In 2024 the oceans took up about 3.4 billion tonnes of carbon and the land about 1.9 billion (Global Carbon Budget), together roughly 19 billion tonnes of CO₂. Deliberate removal of all kinds is about 2 billion tonnes a year (State of CDR, 2024), almost all from planting forests; **novel methods such as DACCS, BECCS and biochar removed about 1.3 million tonnes**, under a thousandth of that. Scenarios that limit warming to 1.5 °C need roughly 7–9 billion tonnes a year of removal by mid-century.

## How long does it stay locked away?

<figure class="learn-figure"><a href="{{ '/assets/learn/storage-durations.svg' | relative_url }}" title="Open the chart full size"><img src="{{ '/assets/learn/storage-durations.svg' | relative_url }}" alt="Typical storage times on a log scale: trees and new woodland, soil carbon and restored peat and wetlands, decades to centuries; biochar, centuries to millennia; rock weathering and mineralisation, bioenergy with carbon capture and storage, and direct air capture with geological storage, 10,000 years or more." loading="lazy"></a><figcaption>Fossil carbon lasts for millennia in the air; only some stores match it. Tap or click to open it full size.</figcaption></figure>

Fossil CO₂ affects the climate for thousands of years. A tree that stores it for 80 years and then burns does not cancel it; it delays it. That is why the UK's plan to admit removals into the UK Emissions Trading Scheme sets a **minimum storage period of 200 years**, and why durable removals are treated differently from forestry credits (see [Offsets: the good, the ugly and the harmful]({{ '/learn/offsets/' | relative_url }})).

## Why it matters in Northern Ireland

- **No cluster, no pipeline.** Northern Ireland is not in either UK track. The Climate Change Committee said in March 2025 that NI “lacks direct access to suitable storage sites”, assumed CCS would not be available in NI before 2035, and recommended the Executive produce a CCS strategy covering cement, energy from waste and removals. CO₂ would probably have to be shipped to a GB cluster or piped to sites in Irish waters.
- **Rocks with potential, not proof.** A 2008 assessment for the island of Ireland, co-commissioned by GSNI, identified sandstones that might hold CO₂: about 37 million tonnes in closed structures in the Portpatrick Basin under the North Channel, and a much larger theoretical figure in Permian sandstones under the Lough Neagh Basin. These are paper estimates, not proven stores, and I have found no newer GSNI assessment.
- **Removals will be needed.** The CCC's pathway for NI relies on engineered removals, mostly direct air capture, to balance the farm emissions that cannot be cut by 2050.
- **Cement and lime.** NI's cement and lime kilns have process emissions that only capture can address. Padeswood in Flintshire, billed by Heidelberg Materials as the UK's first cement plant with carbon capture (final investment decision 25 September 2025, about 800,000 tonnes a year, commissioning planned for 2029), shows what that looks like.

### Think of a bath with the taps running

The atmosphere is a bath. Emissions are the taps; natural sinks and removals are the plughole.

| Bath | Climate |
|---|---|
| Taps running hard | Fossil and land-use emissions, about 42 billion tonnes of CO₂ in 2024 |
| A plughole that drains about half | Oceans and land |
| A teaspoon bailing water out | Novel engineered removal, about 1.3 million tonnes a year |
| Fitting a filter on the tap | CCS on a factory chimney |
| Bailing with a bucket, then pouring it back in | A forest credit that later burns |

Turn the taps down first. Bail only what you cannot turn off.

## Examples

<div class="example" markdown="1">
<p class="sector">Construction</p>

### A block maker using recycled CO₂

A concrete products plant is offered blocks cured with captured CO₂ that mineralises inside them.

| Question | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Where did the CO₂ come from? | Fossil CO₂ from a factory is a reduction at best; biogenic or air-captured CO₂ can be a removal |
| How much is locked in per block? | Usually small next to the cement's own process CO₂ |
| How long does it stay? | As carbonate, effectively permanently |
| Who verified the figure? | Claims need a third-party check |

**The lesson:** CO₂-cured concrete can cut the footprint, but it rarely makes the block carbon negative. The cement content is still the bigger lever.
</div>

<div class="example" markdown="1">
<p class="sector">Agriculture and food</p>

### A farm spreading basalt or biochar

A farmer is offered payment for spreading crushed basalt (enhanced rock weathering) or biochar, with the removals sold as credits.

**What to check:** how the removal is measured, not just modelled; who owns the credits; whether the land and soils are suitable; and the transport emissions of moving rock. The IPCC rates both methods as long-lasting (biochar for centuries to millennia, mineral weathering for 10,000 years or more), but measurement in the field is still hard.

**The lesson:** promising, permanent, and early. Sign nothing that gives away the land's carbon rights for decades without legal advice.
</div>

<div class="example" markdown="1">
<p class="sector">Public sector</p>

### A council's energy-from-waste plant

A council's waste is burned for power. Part of the carbon in mixed waste is biogenic (food, paper, wood) and part fossil (mostly plastics); roughly half and half is a common rule of thumb.

**What capture would do:** capturing the CO₂ would cut the fossil emissions and turn the biogenic half into a removal, which is why the CCC lists energy from waste among NI's CCS needs. Without a CO₂ network, that is a 2035-or-later option.

**The lesson:** keep plastic out of the waste stream now; capture may come later.
</div>

## Common mistakes

1. **Calling CCS on a gas plant “negative”.** It reduces emissions; only removal takes CO₂ out of the air.
2. **Counting captured as stored.** Boundary Dam in Canada has captured 7.3 million tonnes since 2014 (SaskPower), about two-thirds of what a design of roughly 1 million tonnes a year implies; Petra Nova in Texas sent its CO₂ to pump more oil. Judge projects on tonnes stored against design.
3. **Treating a tree as a permanent store.** Forests last decades to centuries and can burn.
4. **Assuming removal will be cheap and plentiful.** Novel removal is about 1.3 million tonnes a year worldwide.
5. **Mixing up absorb and adsorb.** Into the liquid, or onto the surface.
