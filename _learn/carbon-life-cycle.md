Everything we make releases carbon more than once: when the raw materials are dug up or grown, when they are processed, when the product is moved, used and repaired, and when it is thrown away. A life cycle assessment adds those stages up. Done honestly, it shows where most of the carbon sits, which is often not where people expect.

## The journey in six stages

<figure class="learn-figure"><img src="{{ '/assets/learn/life-cycle-loop.svg' | relative_url }}" alt="Six stages in a circle, each releasing carbon: extract (mining, quarrying, felling, farming); make (refining, smelting, manufacture); move (shipping, haulage, delivery); use (installation, running, upkeep); end of life (demolition, landfill, burning); recover (reuse, repair, recycling), which feeds materials back to make. The arrow from recover back to make is dashed because most products never complete the loop." loading="lazy"><figcaption>The dashed arrow is the step most products miss.</figcaption></figure>

1. **Extract:** mining ore, quarrying stone, felling trees, growing crops.
2. **Make:** refining, smelting, firing, manufacturing.
3. **Move:** shipping, haulage and delivery between each step.
4. **Use:** installing it, running it, and maintaining and repairing it.
5. **End of life:** demolition, landfill, incineration.
6. **Recover:** reuse, repair and recycling, which sends materials back round.

## The same journey in the standards

For buildings and construction products, the European standards split the life cycle into lettered modules. BS EN 15978:2026 covers whole buildings and BS EN 15804 covers construction products; the RICS whole life carbon standard (2nd edition, in force from 1 July 2024) follows them.

<figure class="learn-figure"><img src="{{ '/assets/learn/life-cycle-stages.svg' | relative_url }}" alt="Six blocks from left to right: A1 to A3 product stage; A4 to A5 construction; B1 to B5 upkeep in use; B6 to B7 energy and water used in operation; C1 to C4 end of life; D reuse and recycling beyond the system boundary, reported separately. Cradle to gate covers A1 to A3. Upfront carbon covers A1 to A5. Embodied carbon covers everything from A to C except B6 and B7. Operational carbon is B6 and B7. Cradle to grave, or whole-life carbon, covers A to C. Adding D gives cradle to cradle." loading="lazy"><figcaption>The words people use for a number depend on which modules it covers.</figcaption></figure>

| Modules | Stage | What it includes |
|---|---|---|
| A1–A3 | Product | Raw materials, transport to the factory, manufacture |
| A4–A5 | Construction | Transport to site, construction or installation |
| B1–B5 | Use: upkeep | Use, maintenance, repair, replacement, refurbishment |
| B6–B7 | Use: running | Energy and water used to operate it |
| C1–C4 | End of life | Demolition, transport, waste processing, disposal |
| D | Beyond the boundary | Reuse, recovery and recycling, reported separately |

## Where you draw the line

A life cycle number always has a boundary. The common names:

- **Cradle to gate:** from raw materials to the factory gate (A1 to A3). This is what most product carbon footprints and Environmental Product Declarations give.
- **Upfront carbon:** cradle to gate plus delivery and construction (A1 to A5). RICS defines it as emissions "up to practical completion".
- **Cradle to grave:** the whole life, A to C. For a building this is whole-life carbon.
- **Cradle to cradle:** cradle to grave plus what is recovered at the end (module D). This is a design idea rather than a standard term; the standards report module D separately and do not deduct it from the total.

The general rules for product life cycles are in ISO 14040 and ISO 14044. ISO 14067 applies them to a product's carbon footprint, and the GHG Protocol's product standard and BSI's PAS 2050 do the same job. The GHG Protocol requires a final product's footprint to be cradle to grave; cradle to gate is allowed for materials and parts whose final use is not known.

## Carbon now, savings later

The life cycle also has a time dimension. Carbon released making something is spent on day one. Savings from using less energy, lasting longer or being recycled arrive later, and only if they happen. A product that is "better over its lifetime" has to survive its lifetime to prove it. That is why the same artificial Christmas tree can be the greener choice in one home and the worse one in another: see the example below and [Deck the halls, check the rules]({{ '/blog/2026/11/deck-the-halls-check-the-rules/' | relative_url }}).

## Examples

<div class="example" markdown="1">
<p class="sector">Retail and the home</p>

### A real or an artificial Christmas tree

| Stage | Artificial tree | Real tree |
|---|---|---|
| Extract and make | PVC and steel, mostly made in China: most of its carbon is spent here, once | Grown over about ten years, absorbing carbon as it grows |
| Move | Shipped once, by sea | Usually trucked from the grower, every year |
| Use | Stored and reused | Used for one season |
| End of life | Hard to recycle, so often landfill in the end | Chipped, burned or landfilled: the choice decides its footprint |

On figures attributed to the Carbon Trust (quoted by the real-tree growers' trade body and not on the Carbon Trust's own site), a 2 m artificial tree is about 40 kg CO2e, a real tree about 3.5 kg if chipped and about 16 kg if landfilled. The artificial tree only overtakes a chipped real tree after about 11 years of use. The Carbon Trust's own advice gives 7 to 20 years.

**The lesson:** for the artificial tree, the carbon is up front and the saving depends on how long you keep it. For the real tree, the end of life decides the answer.
</div>

<div class="example" markdown="1">
<p class="sector">Construction</p>

### A timber-frame wall or a block wall

How the two compare depends on where you draw the line, not just on the materials.

| Stage | Timber-frame wall | Concrete block wall |
|---|---|---|
| A1–A3, product | Usually lower: timber takes less energy to process | Usually higher: cement is carbon-intensive to make |
| Carbon stored in the material | Timber holds carbon absorbed while the tree grew (biogenic carbon). RICS does not count it as a reduction in upfront carbon | Little; concrete reabsorbs a small amount over decades |
| B1–B5, upkeep | Depends on cladding, treatment and detailing | Usually low |
| C1–C4, end of life | If burned or landfilled, the stored carbon is released again | Crushed for fill or landfill |
| D, beyond the boundary | Reuse of timber, or energy from burning it | Recycled aggregate |

These are general patterns, not measured figures: the real numbers come from the EPDs for the specific products and a whole-life assessment of the specific building.

**The lesson:** a wall that wins on upfront carbon can lose some of its lead at the end of its life, which is why whole-life assessments report the stages separately.
</div>

<div class="example" markdown="1">
<p class="sector">Food and drink</p>

### A litre of milk from a County Antrim farm

| Stage | Where the carbon comes from |
|---|---|
| Extract (the farm) | Methane from the cows' digestion, manure, nitrous oxide from fertilised soil, and the carbon in making the fertiliser and feed |
| Make | Pasteurising, bottling, and making the bottle |
| Move | Collection by tanker, delivery to shops |
| Use | Refrigeration in the shop and at home |
| End of life | Wasted milk, and the bottle in the recycling or the bin |

For most dairy products, life cycle studies generally find that the farm stage accounts for most of the footprint; the share varies from farm to farm. That is why processors and supermarkets ask farmers, not dairies, for most of the data. The fertiliser part links to the carbon border taxes: the emissions from making it are counted under CBAM, but the nitrous oxide from spreading it is the farm's own.

**The lesson:** look for the biggest stage first. Cutting packaging is visible; cutting fertiliser use and feed emissions usually matters more.
</div>

## Common mistakes

1. **Comparing numbers with different boundaries,** such as one supplier's cradle-to-gate figure against another's cradle-to-grave.
2. **Ignoring the end of life.** For trees, timber and anything that can be burned or landfilled, it can change the answer.
3. **Deducting recycling (module D) from the total.** Report it separately.
4. **Treating a future saving as already banked.** A product that pays back over ten years must last ten years.
5. **Measuring the easy stage.** Packaging and transport are easy to see; the biggest stage is often extraction or manufacture.
